## Trinity: one God, real distinction {#orient}
> Historic Christianity confesses one God and three distinct persons. It does not mean three gods, three pieces, or one person in three costumes.

**Canonical Shelf’s own position.** The historic confession described here is what most churches teach, and this guide teaches it accurately so you can recognize and discuss it. Canonical Shelf’s own Statement of Faith takes a different view: it affirms one God with one divine consciousness, and reads Father, Son, and Holy Spirit as Scripture’s language for how that one God relates to creation, not as three separate divine consciousnesses or a literal family tree. It affirms that the divine reality embodied in Jesus existed eternally within God and took part in creation, and that the Holy Spirit is God’s own presence throughout creation. Because this differs from the classical formulation, the guide labels it as its own position rather than presenting it as what all Christians believe.

The closing commission names Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together in baptism. Read what the passage says before moving to later terminology. Historic Trinitarian teaching draws this testimony together with other biblical passages rather than pretending a complete later definition appears in one sentence.

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}
The historic confession holds that God is one and that Father, Son, and Spirit are distinct persons. Person here does not simply mean three separate human individuals enlarged to cosmic size. The language protects distinction without dividing God into three competing beings.

Calling each person one third of God fails to express the confession. Describing one person changing costumes also fails, because it removes real distinction. These are useful near-miss comparisons: identifying why an explanation fails can be clearer than adding an elaborate analogy.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}
A beginner can learn the boundaries of the teaching without claiming to make God fully comprehensible. Mystery names the limits of understanding; it should not be used to avoid distinguishing a coherent formulation from its alternatives. The mission checks that distinction, not personal assent.
