## The garden: freedom, trust, and limits {#orient}
> The story joins a gift, a responsibility, and a relationship. Freedom matters because our actions affect that relationship.

The garden is a place of provision and work. The human receives a task before the story of disobedience: cultivating and keeping what God has provided. Work itself is therefore not introduced here as a punishment. The setting holds receiving and responsibility together.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}
The permission to eat is broad, but it includes a boundary. In the story, freedom is exercised within a relationship of trust; it does not mean every possible action has the same consequence. The warning about death introduces the seriousness of that relationship without explaining every biological mechanism.

The account also identifies isolation as a problem. The arrival of another human is celebrated as shared humanity and relationship. The narrative’s language should be examined carefully before being made into a complete rule for every modern household or a ranking of human worth.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}
Read this scene before the rupture in chapter 3. Notice what can be lost: trust, shared life, access, and a sustaining home. Keeping that earlier goodness in view prevents an introduction to sin from making failure the whole definition of a person.
