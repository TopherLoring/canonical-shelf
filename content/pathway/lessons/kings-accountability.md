## Kingship: evaluating power rather than admiring it {#orient}
> The warning asks what power takes from people. Evaluate consequences as well as promises.

Samuel’s warning repeatedly describes what a king will take. People, produce, and land become resources for royal purposes. The repetition directs attention to the costs of concentrated power rather than only to its promises of stability or prestige.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}
The people still ask for a king, including the desire to resemble other nations and have leadership in battle. The narrative lets competing desires remain visible. A request can be understandable while carrying consequences its supporters would prefer not to notice.

Later biblical accounts include hopes for righteous kings as well as severe criticism of rulers. This warning should be read within that tension. It does not turn every modern institution into a biblical equivalent, nor does a ruler’s appearance in Scripture certify their conduct.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}
Apply the reading by asking concrete questions about power: who benefits, who supplies the labor, who bears the loss, and how decisions can be challenged. Evaluating a leader requires more than admiration for strength or identification with a favored group.
