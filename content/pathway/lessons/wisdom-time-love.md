## Ecclesiastes and Song: limits, delight, and poetry {#orient}
> Wisdom poetry can explore what we cannot control and what we can delight in. An image or listed season is not automatically an instruction.

This lesson closes the unit with two of the Bible’s most surprising books: Ecclesiastes, which questions what lasts, and Song of Songs, which celebrates love and desire.

Ecclesiastes arranges contrasting times in pairs. The poem directs attention to the range of human experience and the limits of control. Its list is not a command that every person must initiate every named action.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Read the poem of times in Ecclesiastes 3:1–8 as pairs, and notice that the poem describes the range of life rather than telling you what to do.

## Questions about what endures {#what-endures}
The book asks difficult questions about work, gain, loss, and what endures. Read its observations within the larger inquiry rather than using one line to declare every action pointless. Wisdom can unsettle easy promises of control while still directing attention to the life actually available.

## Song of Songs: delight and desire {#song}
Song of Songs gives biblical space to desire, delight, and the voices of lovers. Read Song 2:8–13 in the additional passage. Its springtime imagery works poetically; the reading need not choose between denying embodied affection and turning each flower into a separate secret code.

## Allegorical readings {#allegory}
Jewish and Christian readers have also developed allegorical readings of Song. These should be identified as interpretations rather than quietly substituted for the poem’s language. Together, these wisdom books widen an introduction beyond rules and timelines to include limitation, affection, beauty, and attentive reading.

## What you now know {#summary}
Ecclesiastes names the range of human experience and the limits of control without declaring life pointless; Song of Songs gives Scripture room for embodied love and delight. Both widen the Bible beyond rules and certainties.

Jewish and Christian traditions have long read Song of Songs allegorically, of God and Israel or Christ and the Church; most modern scholars read it first as love poetry. The guide presents both and labels the allegorical readings as interpretation. The question to carry forward: in the next unit the prophets speak into crisis; how do they combine judgment and hope?
