## Navigate a library {#orient}
> Think of a Bible reference as an address inside a library. First find the book, then chapter, then verse. Next ask what kind of writing you are reading.

The Bible is a collection of writings composed and preserved across many centuries. The Old Testament includes Israel’s scriptures; the New Testament includes four Gospels, Acts, letters, and Revelation. Christianity emerged within Judaism. The Old Testament is not merely a failed preliminary version of Christianity.

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}
A reference is an address. Luke 1:1–4 means the book of Luke, chapter 1, verses 1 through 4. Chapter and verse divisions help navigation; they do not replace the original flow of an argument or story. Read surrounding paragraphs when investigating a verse.

Canon means the collection a religious community recognizes as Scripture. This reader contains the 66-book Protestant canon in the Berean Standard Bible. Catholic and Orthodox collections include additional books, and Orthodox canons also vary. A translation changes language; it does not by itself define which books belong.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}
Luke begins by describing earlier accounts, transmitted testimony, and his own investigation. That introduction helps us ask questions about sources, purpose, and audience. Narrative, poetry, law, wisdom, letters, and apocalyptic visions call for different reading practices. They can convey truth without all functioning as modern scientific reports.
