## Find your way {#orient}
> Think of a Bible reference as an address inside a library. First find the book, then chapter, then verse. Next ask what kind of writing you are reading.

The last two lessons gave you the shelves and the kinds of writing on them. This lesson answers a practical question: how do you find a passage, and what do you need to know about the Bible in your hands before you trust what you read there? It covers three things every reader meets in the first week: references, the canon, and translation.

The Bible is a collection of writings composed and preserved across many centuries. The Old Testament includes Israel’s scriptures; the New Testament includes four Gospels, Acts, letters, and Revelation. Christianity emerged within Judaism. The Old Testament is not merely a failed preliminary version of Christianity.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Luke opens his Gospel by telling his reader how the book was made: others had written accounts, witnesses had handed on what they saw, and Luke investigated carefully before writing an orderly account. Keep that in mind as you read the rest of this lesson. The Bible itself is aware that it was written, gathered, and passed on by people.

## Read a reference like an address {#references}
A reference is an address. Luke 1:1–4 means the book of Luke, chapter 1, verses 1 through 4. Chapter and verse divisions help navigation; they do not replace the original flow of an argument or story. Read surrounding paragraphs when investigating a verse.

You will see the same reference written in different styles: Luke 1:1–4 in most English Bibles, Lk 1:1–4 where book names are abbreviated, and Luke 1.1–4 in British and academic writing, which uses a period between chapter and verse. They all point to the same four verses. When a reference names a single verse, it is worth opening a few verses before and after it, because a sentence on its own can say something its context does not.

## What is in the collection, and in what language {#canon}
Canon means the collection a religious community recognizes as Scripture. This reader contains the 66-book Protestant canon in the Berean Standard Bible. Catholic and Orthodox collections include additional books, and Orthodox canons also vary. A translation changes language; it does not by itself define which books belong.

Two Bibles can therefore differ for different reasons. One may include books the other does not, which is a question of canon. Two may phrase the same verse differently, which is a question of translation. And a single verse may read differently in two editions because translators weighed the ancient manuscripts differently. None of these differences means that one Bible is fake. They are ordinary features of a collection copied, gathered, and translated over many centuries, and later lessons show how scholars compare them.

## Ask who wrote it, for whom, and how {#setting}
Luke begins by describing earlier accounts, transmitted testimony, and his own investigation. That introduction helps us ask questions about sources, purpose, and audience. Narrative, poetry, law, wisdom, letters, and apocalyptic visions call for different reading practices. They can convey truth without all functioning as modern scientific reports.

These three questions travel with you through the rest of the Pathway: which collection am I reading from, which translation, and what kind of writing is this? Asking them first keeps a reader from treating a difference in wording as a difference in Scripture, or a poem as a report.

## What you now know {#summary}
You can read a reference as an address, open the surrounding passage before drawing a conclusion, and tell apart three kinds of difference between Bibles: which books are included (canon), how the language is rendered (translation), and what kind of writing a passage is (genre).

Christians agree that Scripture was written, gathered, and transmitted by people; they differ on which books belong in the Old Testament and on how to weigh manuscript differences. Those debates come back in Module 3, where manuscripts and translation are taught in detail. The question to carry forward: when two Bibles read differently, which of the three kinds of difference is it?
