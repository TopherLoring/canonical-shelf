## Difficult ethical texts: neither avoidance nor easy excuses {#orient}
> Read what the text says and what it leaves unresolved. Understanding an ancient setting does not require excusing its harms.

Paul appeals to Philemon concerning Onesimus through the language of love, relationship, and reception as a brother. Read the appeal’s actual wording rather than immediately replacing it with either a modern abolition statement or a defense of slavery.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}
The letter operates within a world in which slavery existed. That historical fact helps explain the situation but does not make slavery morally acceptable. Context explains a setting; it is not a machine for turning harm into goodness.

The appeal can be examined for how it changes the relationship it addresses and for what it leaves unstated. Christians have used biblical texts in opposing ways concerning slavery. A responsible reading must confront the harm of oppressive uses rather than claiming the interpretive history is uncomplicated.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}
Use the same method with conquest and restrictions on women: record explicit claims, examine context and competing readings, name the moral problem, and evaluate an application through Jesus’ teaching and human dignity. Some questions remain difficult after careful study. Honesty about those limits is part of the work.
