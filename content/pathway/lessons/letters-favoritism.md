## Letters: reading a correction as a correction {#orient}
> A letter can show a bad practice to challenge it. Look for the author’s judgment, not just the scene being described.

James imagines or describes an assembly giving different treatment to people on the basis of visible wealth. The contrast is vivid enough to reveal how ordinary gestures can establish who is valued. A seating decision can express a social judgment.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}
The letter criticizes favoritism rather than recommending the scene. This is a basic but important reading distinction: Scripture may describe a harmful practice in order to confront it. Isolating the scene from the author’s evaluation reverses the argument.

The appeal to loving one’s neighbor gives the criticism its ethical direction. The issue is not solved by a declaration that everyone is welcome if actual conduct still honors wealth and humiliates poverty. The stated principle must be examined against observable practice.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}
When reading other letters, identify the addressee, the issue, the reasons, and the requested response. A correction tells us something about a problem, but not necessarily its prevalence in every early Christian community. Stay within the evidence before generalizing.
