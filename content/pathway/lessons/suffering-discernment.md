## Suffering, miracles, and spiritual discernment {#orient}
> A tragedy does not prove its victims were worse people. Care can begin before every theological question is answered.

Jesus challenges the inference that people who suffered particular disasters were worse sinners than others. The examples include human violence and a falling tower. The passage does not give a complete theory of suffering, but it directly resists a particular way of blaming victims.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}
This guide distinguishes moral harm arising through human choices from disease and disasters within a dynamic natural world. God’s ability to bring good from suffering does not mean every tragedy was desired or required. Love, freedom, lament, and responsible care remain part of the discussion.

Biblical miracle and deliverance accounts deserve attention to their narrative claims and purposes. Christians differ over personal, symbolic, and systemic readings of spiritual evil. The course does not treat a modern illness as evidence of possession or an unhealed condition as evidence of inadequate faith.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}
When someone is suffering, respond first with attention to immediate needs, listening, and appropriate help. Leave room for prayer and unanswered questions. Do not impose an explanation of God’s hidden intention or let a spiritual label displace practical care.
