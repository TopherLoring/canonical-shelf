## Return: hope becomes the work of rebuilding {#orient}
> A door opens, people respond, and others supply resources. Rebuilding still requires shared work.

Ezra opens with a proclamation allowing the rebuilding of the temple in Jerusalem. The story places political action within its account of God’s purposes. It does not require readers to regard every action of the ruler as morally approved.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}
The return involves people responding and neighbors supplying practical resources. Hope becomes a journey and a building task. A decree can open a possibility without completing the human work that follows from it.

Return should not be confused with a simple restoration of everything lost. Read further in Ezra to encounter conflict and questions about identity and belonging. This opening is the beginning of a difficult process, not a guarantee that a community’s future will be uncomplicated.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}
A contemporary application can distinguish an opportunity from its fulfillment. When rebuilding after disruption, people need resources, participation, and attention to those affected. The analogy is useful without turning a personal recovery story into the exact fulfillment of this ancient proclamation.
