## Exodus: hearing suffering and answering a call {#orient}
> The call starts by taking suffering seriously. Moses is invited to participate even while asking honest questions.

Module 2 begins with the Exodus, one of the central stories shaping Israel’s identity. This lesson reads the call of Moses.

God’s speech begins with the suffering of the people: seeing, hearing, and knowing their affliction. Liberation is introduced through attention to oppression. The story gives the enslaved community’s experience a central place rather than describing their suffering as an inconvenience for rulers.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Notice the verbs in God’s speech before any instruction is given: seen, heard, known, come down. Then notice Moses’ two questions, “Who am I?” and “What is your name?”

## Called with questions {#called-with-questions}
Moses is called into the response, and he asks questions about his role. Divine initiative and human participation are not presented as alternatives. A task can be significant while the person asked to undertake it remains uncertain and in need of support.

## Presence and the divine name {#presence-and-name}
The assurance of divine presence answers Moses’ concern about adequacy. The passage also introduces God’s self-identification and connection with the ancestors. Treat the divine name reverently and attend to translation notes; one English rendering does not exhaust its linguistic or theological significance.

## Reading Exodus responsibly today {#today}
Exodus can shape a Christian concern for people under oppression without making every current conflict a one-to-one reenactment. Identify the specific harm, listen to those experiencing it, and consider responsible participation. Quoting liberation language cannot excuse ignoring evidence or the agency of those being helped.

## What you now know {#summary}
The Exodus begins with God attending to the suffering of an enslaved people, and with a hesitant person called into the response. Divine initiative and human participation work together, and the assurance Moses receives is presence, not certainty about his own adequacy.

This passage foregrounds the suffering of the enslaved people. Christians differ on how directly Exodus should shape modern politics. The guide reads it as forming concern for people under oppression without treating every conflict as a reenactment. The question to carry forward: what does deliverance require after the escape?
