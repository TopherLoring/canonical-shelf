## Persian Judea: return without independence

Lesson ID: c3-persian-judea

**Objective:** Locate postexilic Judah within the Persian Empire and distinguish return, rebuilding, and communal restoration from political independence.

**Primary reading:** Ezra 1:1–4

### Persian Judea: return without independence

The return from exile begins a restoration of Judean community life, but it does not restore an independent kingdom. To see that distinction, begin with the change of empire. Babylon falls to Persia, and the political possibilities for displaced Judeans change with it. Persian rule permits forms of restoration for displaced populations and their places of worship. Ezra presents Cyrus authorizing the rebuilding of Jerusalem’s sanctuary within that imperial setting.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Ezra 1:1–4 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Return describes an actual movement of people, not the relocation of every Judean family in Babylon. Jewish communities continue outside the land. Those who rebuild in Judah also remain subject to Persian authority. Restoration and imperial dependence therefore exist together; the permission to rebuild a sanctuary is not the restoration of the Davidic monarchy.

This combination helps explain the institutions that become important after exile. The rebuilt Temple, priestly leadership, Torah-centered communal life, ancestry, and questions of belonging provide ways to organize a community without an independent monarchy. Ezra and Nehemiah tell this history with theological purposes. Their narratives explain the community’s relationship with God as well as its circumstances, so we should read them as more than administrative records.

### Keep the setting visible

The Persian province is our starting point for the Second Temple period, the long era associated with Jerusalem’s rebuilt sanctuary. Diaspora means communities living outside their ancestral homeland, and it remains part of Jewish life after return begins. Keep both the restored community and the dispersed communities on the map. Several centuries of political and cultural change still separate this scene from Jesus under Rome. The question to carry forward is how these communities sustain their identity while their imperial circumstances change.

### Glossary

### Additional questions

#### Why “return” is not the end of diaspora

Many Jewish communities remain or develop outside Judea. Diaspora becomes a durable feature of Jewish history rather than a temporary mistake corrected by one migration.

### Deeper reading note

Historians reconstruct Persian-period Judah from biblical texts, Persian evidence, archaeology, epigraphy, and comparative imperial history. The biblical narratives have theological interests that should be respected rather than mistaken for modern historiography.

## Torah and public identity after exile

Lesson ID: c3-torah-public-life

**Objective:** Explain how public reading, interpretation, worship, and communal identity become visible in postexilic biblical narratives.

**Primary reading:** Nehemiah 8:1–8

### Torah and public identity after exile

Nehemiah 8 brings us to a public assembly where Torah is read and explained. Torah here is the Scripture around which the community gathers. The lesson’s question is how reading, understanding, worship, and belonging work together. Questions about the books’ composition and dating remain, but the narrative itself clearly presents these activities as connected parts of postexilic Jewish life.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Nehemiah 8:1–8 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

A written text does not remove the need for interpretation. In this scene, people hear the text read aloud and receive help understanding its sense. Public reading makes Scripture available to the assembly; explanation helps the assembly understand what it hears. The narrative therefore places interpretive work inside the reception of Scripture rather than treating it as an obstacle to receiving Scripture.

Torah is important within a larger world of worship, ancestry, land, imperial power, and Temple institutions. These features shape how the community understands its obligations and its boundaries. Sharing them does not guarantee that every Jewish community will interpret them identically. Later communities can agree that Torah matters while disagreeing about what fidelity to it requires.

### Keep the setting visible

That distinction between shared identity and differing interpretation prepares us for the Gospels. Pharisees, Sadducees, priests, teachers, diaspora communities, and other groups participate in overlapping Jewish worlds. They are not separate religions simply because they argue over shared texts and practices. Nehemiah’s assembly shows Scripture being received through reading and explanation; it should not be treated as a fully developed first-century synagogue service. As later institutions appear, ask how they continue this work and where their circumstances differ.

#### Why this matters for synagogues

Later synagogue life includes Scripture reading and teaching, but the institutional history is complex. Do not simply label Nehemiah 8 a fully developed first-century synagogue service.

### Deeper reading note

Scholars debate the relationship between Ezra-Nehemiah’s literary presentation and historical reconstruction. The lesson uses the text to identify a real conceptual development: Scripture is received through communal reading and explanation.

## Alexander and Hellenization: a new cultural world

Lesson ID: c3-alexander-hellenization

**Objective:** Explain how Alexander’s conquests and Hellenistic culture reshape the eastern Mediterranean without imagining that local Jewish identity simply disappears.

**Primary reading:** No primary reading is assigned in the existing source.

### Alexander and Hellenization: a new cultural world

Hellenization means the spread and local adaptation of Greek language and cultural forms. It names a process, not a moment when everyone exchanges one identity for another. In the late fourth century BCE, Alexander of Macedon conquers the Persian Empire and brings Judea into a new political world. After his death in 323 BCE, successor kingdoms compete for territory, including Judea. Political change supplies the setting in which Greek cultural influence spreads.

### Locate the claim in context

Greek influence reaches language, institutions, education, art, politics, and cultural practice. Jewish communities respond to these influences in different ways. Using Greek or participating in civic life does not by itself mean abandoning Jewish identity. The important question is how people combine, accept, resist, or adapt particular practices within their commitments.

Greek becomes especially significant across the eastern Mediterranean. Jewish Scriptures are translated into Greek over time, producing the tradition conventionally called the Septuagint. That name can sound like one collection translated at one moment, but the books’ translation histories develop over time. Greek-speaking Jewish communities are consequently part of the world from which early Christianity emerges, not outsiders to Jewish life because of their language.

### Keep the setting visible

Cultural exchange can bring opportunity and creativity as well as conflict over fidelity. This explains why the Maccabean crisis needs more than a simple opposition between two sealed cultures. Jewish communities already participate in a changing Greek-speaking world, and disagreements concern particular political and religious choices within it. Keep those varied responses in view as the next lesson turns from broad cultural change to a specific crisis.

### Additional questions

Greek-speaking Jews appear throughout the ancient Mediterranean. Language use and religious identity are different dimensions.

#### Why this matters for the New Testament

The New Testament is written in Greek and often engages forms of Greek Jewish Scripture. Course 5 returns to quotation and translation relationships.

### Deeper reading note

“The Septuagint” is a convenient label but can imply more uniformity than the evidence warrants. Different books were translated at different times and textual histories are complex.

## Antiochus IV and the Maccabean crisis

Lesson ID: c3-antiochus-maccabees

**Objective:** Describe the second-century BCE crisis around Antiochus IV, Jewish resistance, and Temple rededication while distinguishing history from later literary interpretation.

**Primary reading:** Daniel 11:29–35

### Antiochus IV and the Maccabean crisis

The Maccabean crisis connects imperial coercion, conflict within Judea, and the fate of Jerusalem’s Temple. In the second century BCE, the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes becomes involved in Judean conflict and imposes measures that ancient Jewish sources describe as attacks on Jewish worship and law. The Temple is profaned. Because the sanctuary is central to worship and communal identity, this becomes a defining crisis rather than merely a change of government.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Daniel 11:29–35 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Resistance associated with Mattathias and his sons, especially Judas Maccabeus, develops into revolt. Calling this simply a war of Jews against Greeks hides part of the explanation. Seleucid power matters, but so do political and cultural conflicts among Jews. The sources describe a struggle within a community as well as a struggle against imperial coercion.

The Temple is rededicated in 164 BCE. Hanukkah later commemorates this rededication, preserving the restoration of worship as a major communal memory. The revolt also contributes eventually to Hasmonean rule. The books of 1 and 2 Maccabees provide major accounts of this history. They can serve as historical sources even though they are outside the 66-book Protestant canon used in this reader. Historical usefulness and canonical status are different questions.

### Keep the setting visible

Many historical-critical interpreters connect important portions of Daniel’s visions with this Antiochene crisis. Other theological readings extend their significance beyond it. The connection helps us understand how symbolic visions can address actual oppression and divine justice. It does not make the visions a code for reading modern newspapers. Keep the historical crisis, the literary presentation, and later interpretation distinguishable as we follow resistance into government.

**Hanukkah.** Hanukkah is the Jewish festival that commemorates the Temple’s rededication after the Maccabean crisis.

### Additional questions

#### Why use sources outside the Protestant canon?

Historical study can use 1–2 Maccabees, Josephus, inscriptions, archaeology, and other sources without treating all of them as Scripture in a Protestant canon.

#### Daniel and historical interpretation

Connecting Daniel 7–12 with second-century events is a mainstream historical-critical approach. Other theological readings extend the visions beyond that crisis; Course 5 distinguishes literary setting, historical reconstruction, and later interpretation.

### Deeper reading note

Ancient sources differ in emphases and explanations for the crisis. Political rivalry, Hellenization, imperial coercion, priestly conflict, and religious persecution interact. A responsible introduction avoids reducing the conflict to one cause.

## Hasmonean rule: resistance becomes a dynasty

Lesson ID: c3-hasmoneans

**Objective:** Explain how the Maccabean revolt develops into Hasmonean political and priestly rule and why that history matters for later Jewish politics.

**Primary reading:** No primary reading is assigned in the existing source.

### Hasmonean rule: resistance becomes a dynasty

The Hasmoneans are the ruling house that develops from the Maccabean movement. Their history asks what changes when resistance becomes government. The revolt against Seleucid control eventually brings Jewish self-rule, but leaders combine military, political, and priestly authority. That combination creates new controversies alongside the achievement of self-government.

### Locate the claim in context

As Hasmonean territory expands, rulers must deal with diplomacy, warfare, competing groups, and the legitimacy of their priestly authority. Legitimacy means a claim to rightful rule. A movement resisting foreign domination now has to explain why its own dynasty should hold power and how its political authority relates to Temple leadership.

This is why later arguments about priesthood, kingship, law, purity, and authority have historical weight. Different Jewish groups remember and evaluate recent rulers differently. Their disagreements concern institutions and decisions they have lived with, rather than only abstract religious principles. Jewish self-rule is significant, but it does not produce agreement about how that rule should operate.

### Keep the setting visible

Internal Hasmonean conflict eventually opens a way for Roman intervention. Pompey enters Jerusalem in 63 BCE, and Judea moves into a new imperial relationship. This transition frames the later world of Herod and the Gospels. Follow the broad movement from crisis to revolt, dynasty, and Roman involvement while remembering that titles, territory, and degrees of independence change within the Hasmonean period. The next question is how Rome exercises authority through this local world.

## Rome enters Judea: empire becomes the political horizon

Lesson ID: c3-rome-judea

**Objective:** Locate Judea and Galilee within Roman imperial power and explain why rulers, taxes, armies, and client kings matter for Gospel reading.

**Primary reading:** Luke 2:1–3

### Rome enters Judea: empire becomes the political horizon

Roman power provides the political setting for the Gospels, but it works through several kinds of authority. Its involvement in Judea begins well before Jesus’ ministry. Pompey enters Jerusalem in 63 BCE, and the region becomes increasingly entangled in Rome’s politics, military power, taxation, and relationships with local dynasties. To read the Gospels carefully, we need to distinguish these relationships rather than giving every ruler the same job.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Luke 2:1–3 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Rome governs through changing, layered arrangements. A client ruler exercises local authority that depends on imperial support. A governor administers a territory under Rome. Local elites and priestly leaders also exercise authority within this larger system. These positions can overlap in influence without becoming identical offices. References to Caesar, Herod, governors, tax collectors, and Temple leaders therefore need to be read according to the kind of power involved.

Imperial rule affects land, revenue, status, public order, and the threat of violence. Families, villages, agriculture, trade, worship, and local institutions nevertheless continue to shape daily life. Describing Roman rule accurately requires both dimensions: the imperial conditions under which people live and the ordinary relationships through which they live.

### Keep the setting visible

In this setting, a claim about kingship, a question about taxes, or a large public gathering can have political consequences. Crucifixion also belongs to the exercise of imperial force. These connections explain why power and public order matter in Gospel scenes. They do not determine the meaning of every saying or turn Jesus into a modern political category. Keep the political setting available, then ask what the particular narrative actually shows.

## Herod the Great: client king, builder, and contested ruler

Lesson ID: c3-herod

**Objective:** Explain Herod’s place between Rome and Judea and recognize how his rule reshapes the Temple and the political background of Gospel birth traditions.

**Primary reading:** Matthew 2:1–8

### Herod the Great: client king, builder, and contested ruler

Herod the Great is a client king: he rules locally, but his authority depends on Roman recognition and support. He governs Judea in the late first century BCE as the head of a dynasty. This makes him different from a Roman governor administering a territory. His place between Rome and Judea explains both his political position and the lasting importance of his building projects.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Matthew 2:1–8 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Herod undertakes ambitious construction, including a massive renovation and expansion of Jerusalem’s Temple complex. He does not establish the Second Temple from nothing. He transforms an existing sanctuary, and Jesus’ generation encounters the Second Temple in the form shaped by that work.

Ancient sources also portray Herod as ruthless and anxious about the security of his dynasty. Matthew’s birth narrative places the royal identity of Jesus in tension with Herod’s rule. That story uses Scripture and theological interpretation to present the conflict. Distinguishing its purposes from those of a modern court chronicle helps us ask what the narrative claims without assuming that its form is a modern biography.

### Keep the setting visible

After Herod’s death, his territories are divided among successors, including Herod Antipas in Galilee. The name Herod in the New Testament can therefore refer to different members of the same ruling family. Identifying the person matters because these successors hold authority in different places and narrative settings. Keep the political, architectural, and dynastic strands together: Rome supports Herod’s kingship, his construction reshapes the Temple, and his family continues into the Gospel world.

## The Second Temple: worship, economy, authority, and pilgrimage

Lesson ID: c3-temple-priesthood

**Objective:** Describe the Second Temple as a religious center embedded in pilgrimage, priesthood, economy, and political authority.

**Primary reading:** Luke 2:41–49

### The Second Temple: worship, economy, authority, and pilgrimage

The Second Temple is a center of worship with material and political consequences. Sacrifice, festivals, pilgrimage, priestly service, teaching, exchange, and public gatherings all belong to its life. Its role in communal identity extends beyond Jerusalem because people elsewhere remain connected to the sanctuary. Understanding these activities together explains why the Temple is central to Gospel narratives rather than simply their background scenery.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Luke 2:41–49 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Priestly families and the high priesthood have responsibilities for worship and leadership. They also work within changing political systems, including foreign imperial rule and Herodian and Roman arrangements. Religious authority can consequently coexist with political entanglement. Recognizing that relationship does not mean that every priest has the same interests or motives.

Pilgrimage festivals bring many people to Jerusalem. Their arrival requires travel, exchange, offerings, and the practical organization of sacrifice, while large crowds raise concerns about public order. Passover is especially significant because it remembers liberation in a community living under imperial power. The festival’s meaning and its circumstances help explain its political sensitivity without telling us what every pilgrim privately intends.

### Keep the setting visible

Jesus’ Temple actions and the passion narratives belong within this combination of worship, pilgrimage, economy, and authority. Read a criticism by asking which practice or leadership decision is challenged. A specific criticism does not establish that Jewish worship as a whole is corrupt or obsolete. Keeping the institution’s many roles visible makes the conflict more intelligible and prevents a local dispute from becoming a sweeping judgment against Judaism.

## Synagogue and diaspora: Jewish life beyond the Temple

Lesson ID: c3-synagogue-diaspora

**Objective:** Explain how synagogue and diaspora settings broaden the learner’s picture of Jewish life beyond Jerusalem sacrifice.

**Primary reading:** Luke 4:16–21

### Synagogue and diaspora: Jewish life beyond the Temple

The Jerusalem Temple has a unique role, but Jewish community life also takes place far beyond it. Synagogues provide settings for assembly, Scripture reading, teaching, prayer, and communal activity in Judea, Galilee, and the diaspora. Diaspora refers to communities living outside an ancestral homeland. These institutions and communities help explain how Jewish life remains connected across a wide geography.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Luke 4:16–21 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Synagogue institutions develop over time, and evidence differs from place to place. We should not assume that every gathering has the same organization. The Gospels and Acts nevertheless present synagogue settings as familiar places for reading, teaching, debate, and community response. Their narratives show why these settings matter without supplying a complete institutional history.

Jewish diaspora communities live throughout the Mediterranean and Near East. Many use Greek extensively while maintaining Scriptures, practices, local communal institutions, and connections with Jerusalem. Language and religious identity are different dimensions of life. Greek usage therefore does not make a community less Jewish or remove its relationship with the Temple.

### Keep the setting visible

This wider geography becomes important when we read Acts and Paul. Early Christian mission often enters cities where Jewish communities already have networks, Scriptures, debates about Gentile participation, and places of encounter. These existing relationships help explain the movement’s routes and discussions. ### Investigate a translation difference

A difference between English Bibles needs a specific question before it needs a verdict. It may concern translation, underlying manuscript wording, or interpretation. Translators work from ancient-language texts and choose how words, sentences, idioms, and style will communicate. A word can have several possible meanings, and copying an expression’s order mechanically may obscure what it means. A translation question asks how to render an underlying expression. A textual question asks which wording the surviving manuscript witnesses best support when they differ. ## Translation spectrum: form, function, and readability

**Objective:** Compare formal and functional translation strategies without ranking an entire Bible by one slogan.

### Translation spectrum: form, function, and readability

Formal and functional equivalence describe priorities within translation. A relatively formal rendering preserves source-language structures or repeated forms where readable English allows it. Each approach involves choices about form, sense, idiom, and readability; no approach removes the need for judgment.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** 1 Corinthians 13:4–7 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

These categories describe tendencies rather than sealed boxes. The same translation can preserve form closely in one verse and recast it in another, because languages do not correspond word for word. Even a very literal rendering must choose meanings, reorder material, or supply words to make grammatical English.

Ask what a particular rendering preserves, clarifies, obscures, or interprets. A literal treatment of an idiom may retain the original image but leave its sense unclear. It does not by itself establish a difference in the underlying manuscript.

For serious study, consult more than one translation and read each translation’s own preface and notes. They help explain its aims and particular decisions. Fulfillment language can describe a promise, pattern, or divine purpose coming to realization. Its literary relationship varies from passage to passage. ### Prediction, typology, quotation, and allusion

## The Synoptic Problem: why Matthew, Mark, and Luke are so similar

Lesson ID: c5-synoptic-problem

**Objective:** Explain the basic Synoptic Problem and distinguish literary dependence hypotheses from claims about fraud or theological unreliability.

**Primary reading:** Mark 1:1–8

### The Synoptic Problem: why Matthew, Mark, and Luke are so similar

Matthew, Mark, and Luke are called the Synoptic Gospels because they overlap substantially and can be compared alongside one another. Their shared material includes close wording, sequence, and story structure. The Synoptic Problem asks how these similarities and differences arose. Scholars consider literary relationships, sources, oral tradition, and editorial choices in proposing explanations. The observed pattern and the hypothesis explaining it should remain distinct.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Mark 1:1–8 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

A widely held model proposes Markan priority: Mark precedes Matthew and Luke, which use it alongside other material. Some models also propose Q to explain material shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark. Q is a hypothetical source, not a surviving manuscript we possess. Its proposed role explains why the term belongs to a model rather than to the direct manuscript evidence.

Other models include versions of the Farrer hypothesis and Griesbach or Two-Gospel approaches. Beginners do not need to choose a winner before reading responsibly. They do need to identify the pattern a source theory explains and where uncertainty remains. Naming alternatives prevents one introductory model from being treated as though it closed every scholarly question.

### Keep the setting visible

Literary dependence does not automatically establish plagiarism or deception under modern academic rules. Ancient authors could reuse and reshape sources, and the meaning of that practice requires historical examination. For reading, the benefit of comparison is noticing what each Gospel preserves, omits, relocates, or emphasizes. Source hypotheses help organize that examination; they do not replace reading the completed accounts on their own terms.

## Authorship, attribution, and ancient composition

Lesson ID: c5-authorship-composition

**Objective:** Distinguish a text’s internal claims, traditional attribution, scholarly reconstruction, use of sources, and ancient compositional practices.

**Primary reading:** Luke 1:1–4

### Authorship, attribution, and ancient composition

Authorship questions require several kinds of evidence. A biblical book need not fit the modern picture of one named person composing alone from a blank page. Ancient works can involve sources, scribes, editors, schools, collected traditions, pseudonymous attribution, and later shaping in different combinations. The task is to distinguish a work’s internal claims, its traditional attribution, and reconstructions of its composition.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Luke 1:1–4 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Begin a book profile with what the text itself says. Luke’s opening describes earlier accounts, transmitted testimony, investigation, and ordered writing. Those four verses do not state the later traditional author name. The account’s internal description and its later title or attribution therefore supply different kinds of evidence.

Traditional attribution remains historically significant, but it should not be renamed an explicit internal claim where the text makes none. A scholarly proposal about editorial stages or composition likewise needs to be identified as reconstruction, with confidence appropriate to its evidence. These distinctions make authorship arguments inspectable instead of allowing one form of evidence to stand silently for another.

### Keep the setting visible

Pseudonymity concerns writing under another name or attributed persona, and its forms and motives are debated differently across ancient collections and biblical books. Its ethical significance should not automatically be equated with modern plagiarism or impersonation without historical argument. Authorship discussions are book-specific. Carry forward a clear distinction among textual claims, traditions, and hypotheses, rather than expecting one generic rule to settle every case.

### Glossary

**Attribution.** Attribution identifies a work with an author, whether through its own claims, tradition, or later scholarship. These kinds of evidence should remain distinct.

**Pseudonymity.** Pseudonymity concerns writing under, or attribution to, a name or persona other than the direct composer. Ancient forms and motives vary.

**Redaction.** Redaction is the editing or shaping of inherited material into a literary work. A proposal about that process needs evidence and an appropriate confidence level.

### Additional questions

#### Confidence labels matter

“Traditional,” “possible,” “probable,” “disputed,” and “unknown” should correspond to actual evidence rather than being rhetorical ways to reward a preferred conclusion.

### Deeper reading note

Authorship debates are book-specific. Evidence can include internal claims, vocabulary, style, historical fit, manuscript titles, patristic testimony, source relationships, and reception. One generic rule cannot settle every biblical book.
