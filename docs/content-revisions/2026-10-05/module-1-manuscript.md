## Begin with the central story

Lesson ID: begin

**Objective:** Put the proclamation Paul recalls into your own words, define its key terms, and tell the difference between understanding Christianity's claim and agreeing with it.

**Primary reading:** 1 Corinthians 15:1–5

### Start with a person and a story
Christianity centers on Jesus of Nazareth: his life, his death, and the proclamation that he died for sins and was raised from the dead. You can study what that claim means before deciding what you believe about it.

Paul passed on a compact summary of this message to a community he knew. It is one of the earliest surviving written statements of the Christian proclamation, and it gives us a starting point for the Learning Path. The shape of the Bible, Christian practices, and the arguments Christians still have all connect with this central story. As you read, notice what Paul says happened and distinguish that claim from the questions you want to ask about it.

### Read the passage
Paul wrote to a Christian community in Corinth around twenty years after Jesus' death. Near the end of his letter, he reminded them of the message he had first brought them. Read the passage slowly and notice what he says happened, rather than trying to resolve every question about Christianity at once.

> **Read:** 1 Corinthians 15:1–5 (BSB).

The summary follows four events: Christ died, was buried, was raised, and appeared. Paul presents them in an order and names witnesses. Burial belongs after death, and the appearances belong after the claim that Christ was raised. The sequence is part of the meaning, rather than a list of interchangeable religious ideas.

Paul also says that he received this message and passed it on. Those words present him as a transmitter of a proclamation already shared among Christians. They help explain why this part of the letter sounds like a summary of something his readers have heard before.

**Check: Put the proclamation in order**

Arrange the four events in the order Paul recalls them.

1. Christ was buried
2. Christ appeared to witnesses
3. Christ died for sins
4. Christ was raised

**Answer order:** Christ died for sins → Christ was buried → Christ was raised → Christ appeared to witnesses

**Explanation:** Paul's summary moves from death to burial to resurrection to appearances. The order matters: the burial confirms the death, and the appearances are offered as evidence for the resurrection.

### What the words mean
Three words help explain the proclamation Paul has just recalled: gospel, Christ, and resurrection. Knowing their meanings makes it easier to say what the passage claims without confusing a title, a message, and an event.

In the New Testament, gospel translates the Greek word euangelion, meaning good news or good announcement. In the ancient world, good-news language could announce a military victory or celebrate a ruler. Such announcements mattered because they concerned events understood to change people's shared world. Paul applies this language to Jesus' death and resurrection, events Christians understand to change humanity's relationship with God. The announcement therefore reaches beyond a hearer's private feelings. The word later also became the name for the first four books of the New Testament that tell Jesus' story: Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John. The message and the books are related uses of the same term.

Christ comes from the Greek word christos, meaning anointed one. It corresponds to the Hebrew title Messiah. Calling Jesus the Christ therefore places him within Israel's hopes for the one God would send; it is not a statement about his family name. That connection with Israel's story will become clearer as you read the rest of the library.

Resurrection means being raised from death to a new, bodily life. In this proclamation it does not mean returning as a ghost, resuming the same mortal life, or being reborn as another person. The distinction matters because Paul is announcing what Christians say happened to Jesus, rather than using death and life as interchangeable images.

**Check: Match the words to their meanings**

### A message inside a letter
These words occur inside a letter to an existing community. Paul is addressing people who already know the message and who have questions and disagreements. He recalls the death, burial, resurrection, and appearances because those claims belong to the proclamation they received together.

The passage is consequently brief. It is not a complete biography of Jesus, and Paul is not trying to tell every episode of his life here. He assumes a shared story and recalls its central events for a particular purpose within the letter. Reading the summary as a biography would leave us wondering why so much is missing; reading it as a reminder helps explain its form.

The same habit will help with other biblical passages. Ask who is speaking, whom they are addressing, and why they are saying this here. A passage written to particular people in particular circumstances can teach later readers, but understanding those circumstances comes before deciding how it applies to us.

### The community behind the letter
The people receiving the letter lived in Roman Corinth. Paul was writing to a community whose members shared meals and relationships, rather than addressing only isolated individuals. In that world, patronage linked resources and assistance with expectations of loyalty and public honor. A gift could also create an obligation.

That background helps us ask who held influence in the community and whose needs were overlooked. It does not tell us the private motive of every Corinthian. We can use a historical setting to ask better questions without pretending that it supplies facts the passage never gives.

Read 1 Corinthians 11:17–22 alongside this lesson. Paul describes some people going hungry while others have plenty, and he rebukes the humiliation of those who have nothing. Those details support a criticism of the community's conduct. They also show why the gospel recalled in chapter 15 concerns shared life as well as what individuals believe.

### How this guide teaches
Canonical Shelf teaches from an explicitly LGBTQ-affirming, open-table perspective, grounded in its published Statement of Faith. It takes historic Christian teaching seriously and identifies where Christians disagree. Stating that perspective lets you examine its reasoning rather than mistaking its teaching for a position without commitments.

You may explore, question, or disagree as you study. The checks ask whether you understand a passage or an argument, not whether you personally accept Christianity or the guide's conclusions. The distinction introduced at the beginning of this lesson remains part of how the whole Learning Path works.

### What you now know
You can now put Paul's proclamation into your own words: Christ died for sins, was buried, was raised, and appeared to witnesses. Gospel names the good news Paul passes on; Christ is a title meaning anointed one; resurrection names being raised to new, bodily life. Paul recalls this message to a real community whose shared conduct also matters to him.

The purpose of this first lesson is orientation. Begin with what the passage says before trying to settle every question about salvation. Later lessons distinguish Christ's saving work, receiving grace through faith and repentance, and the life that follows in response to grace. Those are connected subjects, but this short proclamation is not a full explanation of each one.

Christians have long shared this center while debating how to explain it. What does dying for sins accomplish? What kind of body does resurrection involve? How should the appearances be understood? Later lessons take up those questions with more context. For now, carry forward the question that connects this opening with the next part of the path: how does the rest of the biblical library relate to this central story?

### Glossary

**Gospel.** In the New Testament, gospel translates the Greek word euangelion, meaning good news or good announcement. In the ancient world, good-news language could be used for public events such as victories and celebrations of rulers. Paul uses it for Jesus' death and resurrection: events Christians understand to change humanity's relationship with God. It later also became the name for the four books that tell Jesus' story: Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John.

**Christ.** Christ comes from the Greek christos, meaning anointed one, and corresponds to the Hebrew title Messiah. It names Jesus' role within Israel's hopes, rather than functioning as his surname.

**Resurrection.** Resurrection means being raised from death to new, bodily life. In the Christian proclamation, Jesus is raised rather than merely revived to resume his former life. The term does not mean becoming a ghost or being reborn as someone else.

**Proclamation.** A proclamation is a public announcement. In this passage it is the message about Jesus that Paul received and passed on, rather than an account he presents as his own invention.

**Patronage.** Patronage describes relationships in which people provide resources or assistance and expect loyalty, honor, or other obligations in return. That background helps explain questions about status in a community, but it does not establish every person's motives.

### Additional questions

#### Do I have to believe this to keep going?

No. Every check in this guide asks whether you understood, never whether you agree. You can study the claim, question it, and decide for yourself.

#### Why start with Paul and not with Genesis?

Because this short passage is the earliest written summary of what the first Christians proclaimed. Knowing the center first gives the rest of the library a place to connect.

### Deeper reading note

Paul distinguishes the proclamation from his own role as its messenger. Ask what changes when we read this as a reminder to a community rather than as a modern individual conversion script. Modern individualistic habits may reduce Paul’s letters to “my beliefs” or “my spiritual success.” Ask instead how status, dependence, and public reputation shape a congregation. New Testament scholar Theodore W. Jennings examines patronage—resource relationships shaped by loyalty, honor, and reciprocal obligation—especially in 2 Corinthians 8–9; applying that lens elsewhere is an interpretive proposal, to be checked against each passage. In 1 Corinthians 11, hunger and humiliation are explicit evidence; a precise seating plan or each host’s intention is a reconstruction. Paul’s critique reaches communal conduct, not simply private sincerity.

Paul writes to a community in Roman Corinth, where relationships of patronage traded help for loyalty and public honor. In 1 Corinthians 11:17–22 he rebukes a shared meal at which some go hungry while others have plenty. Reading chapter 15 alongside chapter 11 shows that the message Paul recalls was meant to shape how a community lives together, not only what each person privately believes. How far the patronage lens explains a given passage is an interpretive question to test against each text.

## Questions worth carrying

Lesson ID: c1-questions-first

**Objective:** Explain why Canonical Shelf introduces difficult questions early while delaying stronger conclusions until the learner has enough biblical, historical, and interpretive foundation to evaluate them responsibly.

**Primary reading:** Acts 17:10–12

### Bring your questions
Adults often begin Bible study with questions already in mind. Why does suffering exist? Why did Jesus die? What is sin? Can Scripture be trusted after centuries of copying and translation? Why do Christians disagree about sexuality, women, hell, miracles, predestination, violence, other religions, and which commands still apply? These questions help explain why the curriculum exists.

You will meet them early, receive an initial map, and return to them as you learn the evidence and context needed to weigh stronger answers. Bringing a question now does not mean you must resolve it immediately. It means you can recognize which later passages and ideas bear on it.

The reading from Acts gives us a way to approach that work. Notice how the hearers respond to what they are told and what they do to examine it.

> **Read:** Acts 17:10–12 (BSB).

### Important is not the same as simple
A question can matter deeply and still require more than a short answer. One verse may belong to an ancient language, a literary genre, a covenant, a historical conflict, or a longer argument. Later Christians may also have used it in a doctrinal debate. An answer that ignores those layers can sound confident without explaining the passage well.

Acts describes hearers who receive a claim seriously and examine the Scriptures to evaluate it. Openness and investigation work together here: they listen rather than dismissing the message, and they investigate rather than treating reception as the end of the task. The Learning Path develops that same combination. First identify the question, then learn enough to judge the reasons offered for an answer.

**Answer order:** Encounter the question → Build biblical and historical context → Evaluate interpretation and evidence → Synthesize a conclusion

### How the path answers
The first part of the path gives you orientation and responsible first answers. You meet Christianity's central claim, the questions that worry new readers, the Bible as a library, and the story from creation to Abraham. That map lets you see where a question belongs before you have studied all the material needed to answer it.

The next two parts develop the biblical and historical setting. You read covenant and law, sacrifice and temple, wisdom, exile, and hope. Then you enter the Second Temple world and study Jesus, the cross and resurrection, Acts, Paul, and the early Church. Manuscripts and translation become detailed subjects where the Gospels make their importance clear.

Throughout that work you learn how an interpretation is supported. Genre, word meanings, historical setting, later uses of earlier texts, competing readings, and degrees of confidence all affect the answer. The final part brings those strands together. Difficult questions return with a larger body of evidence and better tools; they have not been hidden until then.

### Holding an answer lightly
A provisional answer is one you can use for now while remaining willing to change it. You may arrive at an initial understanding, study further, and revise your judgment when stronger evidence becomes available. Changing an answer for a reason is part of learning.

This does not require you to postpone every conclusion or assume that all answers are equally uncertain. Some claims are better supported than others. The point is to make your level of confidence fit the evidence you have learned to evaluate. Where you can explain the reasons, you can judge the conclusion more responsibly.

### What you now know
Your questions belong in the study from the beginning. The path introduces them early, provides an initial map, and returns to them as your knowledge of Scripture and its setting grows. An important question may need several kinds of evidence, which explains why the curriculum does not promise an immediate final answer to every concern.

Christians continue to disagree about many of these subjects. Later lessons explain the competing readings and their reasons, rather than assuming that disagreement makes every answer equally persuasive. Carry forward the question you most want to understand, and notice which parts of the path could improve your ability to answer it.

### Reflect
**Optional reflection**

**Model response:** A strong response names a question and identifies at least one kind of background—textual, historical, literary, linguistic, or theological—that could materially improve the answer.

### Glossary

**Provisional.** A provisional conclusion is an answer you have reason to use for now while remaining willing to revise it. Its strength should reflect the evidence you can presently evaluate, rather than the certainty you would like to have.

**Foundation.** A foundation is the background knowledge and reading skill needed to evaluate a question responsibly. In Bible study this may include the passage's language, literary form, historical setting, or place in an argument.

**Synthesis.** Synthesis brings earlier learning and several kinds of evidence together into a connected conclusion. It explains how the pieces support the answer, rather than simply placing them beside one another.

### Additional questions

#### Questions we will return to

God and Trinity; Scripture and trust; sin, salvation, and the cross; suffering and evil; violence, conquest, and slavery; sexuality and LGBTQ interpretation; women and ministry; judgment, hell, and resurrection; other religions and the unevangelized; miracles and historical evidence; Christian disagreement; and law, covenant, and ethics.

#### Does “later” mean “no answer now”?

No. Module 1 gives the central Christian claims, essential distinctions, and responsible first-pass explanations. Later modules deepen or qualify those answers where additional biblical history, cultural context, textual evidence, or competing interpretations materially matter.

#### What if I disagree?

Understanding is not assent. Canonical Shelf can state its own theological/editorial position while still asking you to identify the text, evidence, alternatives, uncertainty, and reasoning involved. Scored work evaluates understanding and reasoning rather than requiring you to agree personally.

### Deeper reading note

Spiral learning deliberately returns to important ideas after the learner has gained new knowledge. Repetition is not the goal by itself; each return should increase what the learner can distinguish, explain, evaluate, or transfer.

## Meet the library: nine kinds of books

Lesson ID: c1-library-groups

**Objective:** Name the nine groups of the 66-book Protestant Bible, say what each group contains, and choose a sensible place to start reading in each.

**Primary reading:** Luke 24:44–45

### A library, not a book
The Bible brings together writings composed over many centuries by many authors. The 66-book Protestant collection used here has two main parts: the Old Testament, containing Israel's Scriptures, and the New Testament, containing the writings of the first Christians. Those books do not all use the same form or ask the same questions.

This library is arranged on nine shelves: Law, History, Wisdom and Poetry, Major Prophets, Minor Prophets, Gospels and Acts, Paul's Letters, General Letters, and Revelation. The shelves help you find a book and form a first expectation about how to read it. They do not replace attention to the passage itself.

In the reading, Jesus speaks of the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms. Notice that Scripture could be described in groups before this particular nine-shelf arrangement.

> **Read:** Luke 24:44–45 (BSB).

### The Old Testament shelves
The Law contains Genesis through Deuteronomy. These books tell Israel's founding story: creation, the ancestors, the Exodus from Egypt, and the covenant at Sinai. The stories provide the setting for the laws, and the laws describe the life of a covenant community in its own world. Reading them well means relating a command to that community rather than copying every rule directly into modern life. Begin with Genesis 1–3 and 12, followed by Exodus 1–3 and 20.

The History books run from Joshua through Esther. They follow Israel in the land through judges, kings, division, exile, and return. Their accounts interpret events as well as reporting them: repeated patterns of faithfulness and failure help explain what the writers think the events mean. Begin with 1 Samuel 16–17, 2 Kings 17, and Nehemiah 8.

Wisdom and Poetry contains Job through Song of Songs. These books bring together prayers, songs, sayings, and reflections on suffering, love, and the way life works. Read slowly enough to notice images and the relationship between paired lines. The second line of a Hebrew poem may deepen the first, so both belong to the meaning. Begin with Psalms 1, 23, and 139, Proverbs 1, and Job 38.

The Major Prophets, Isaiah through Daniel, are longer books. The Minor Prophets, Hosea through Malachi, are twelve shorter books; minor describes their length, not their importance. The prophets address people before, during, and after the exile with warnings, appeals, and promises. Ask whom they address and which injustice they name before looking for predictions. Begin with Isaiah 1 and 40, Jeremiah 31, Amos 5, Micah 6, and the four chapters of Jonah.

### The New Testament shelves
The Gospels and Acts introduce Jesus' life, death, and resurrection and the early Church. The four Gospels share a central story while emphasizing different aspects of it for their readers. Acts continues the account begun in Luke. Begin with Mark, the shortest Gospel, then read Luke 15 and Acts 2.

Paul's Letters are grouped from Romans through Philemon. The General Letters run from Hebrews through Jude and address particular recipients or broader Christian audiences. These writings respond to real questions and problems. Find the writer, the readers, and the situation before extracting a teaching from a sentence. Begin with Philippians, Romans 5–8, James 1–2, and 1 John 4.

Revelation has its own shelf. It uses apocalyptic visions and symbols to address suffering, empire, and God's making all things new. Its images belong to a message for its first readers, so begin by asking what those images meant in their setting before considering their meaning for the future. Revelation 1 and 21–22 provide useful starting places.

### Glossary

**Old Testament.** The Old Testament is the Christian name for the collection of Israel's Scriptures. Protestant Bibles contain 39 books in this collection, written mostly in Hebrew. Jewish readers share these writings but arrange and count them differently, and read them within a living Jewish tradition.

**New Testament.** The New Testament contains 27 writings about Jesus and the early Christian movement, composed in Greek. Its contents include four Gospels, Acts, letters, and Revelation, rather than one continuous account written in a single form.

**Pentateuch.** Pentateuch names the first five books of the Bible: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. This collection is also called the Torah or the Law; its laws occur within Israel's founding story.

**Canon.** A canon is the collection of books a community recognizes as Scripture. Protestant Bibles contain 66 books. Differences between Christian collections concern which writings are included, rather than simply how the same writings are translated.

**Epistle.** An epistle is a letter. Biblical letters address communities or other recipients and often respond to questions, disagreements, or conduct. Knowing the situation helps explain what the letter is doing.

### Additional questions

#### Why nine groups and not two?

Old and New Testament tell you which collection a book belongs to. The nine groups tell you what kind of book you are holding, which changes how you read it.

#### Do I have to read the Bible in order?

No. Many readers start with a Gospel, then Genesis and Exodus, then the Psalms. The shelf helps you know where you are wherever you begin.

#### Are the Apocrypha missing?

Protestant Bibles do not include the additional books found in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles. The extra-credit module covers them, along with how the canon took shape.

### Deeper reading note

The group names used here follow common Protestant arrangement. Jewish Bibles arrange the same Hebrew books differently, into Law, Prophets, and Writings, and Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include additional books. Each book’s profile on the Bible page shows its themes, people, setting, and where to begin.

## How to read what you’re reading

Lesson ID: c1-reading-kinds

**Objective:** Recognize the main kinds of biblical writing, explain what kind of claim each makes, and describe the views Christians hold where they read a passage differently.

**Primary reading:** Nehemiah 8:8

### Every passage is a kind of writing
Understanding a passage includes recognizing how it communicates. A historical account, a love poem, and a teaching story can all concern something true, but they make different kinds of claims. The Bible contains history, compressed summary, law, poetry, wisdom, prophecy, apocalyptic vision, parable, letter, and Gospel. Reading every form as though it were a report would miss how much of that writing works.

Nehemiah describes the Scriptures being read and explained so that the people could understand them. That connection between reading and understanding guides this lesson. We will identify the main forms and the clues that help us recognize what they are doing.

> **Read:** Nehemiah 8:8 (BSB).

### History and summary
Historical narrative names people and places and follows events in a sequence. It presents those events as having happened while selecting and emphasizing details to communicate their meaning. Samuel, Kings, and Acts contain this kind of writing. Selection itself is part of telling a history, rather than evidence that the writer has necessarily distorted it.

Some passages compress much longer stretches of time. Genealogies, brief accounts of a period, and phrases such as in those days provide an outline rather than a complete record of everything that happened. Genesis 5 and 11, Chronicles, and Judges 2 include such summaries. Treating every summary as a complete timeline asks it to supply information its form may not offer.

### Law, poetry, and wisdom
Law communicates through commands and cases, including situations introduced with if a man. It describes how a covenant community should live in its setting. Understanding that setting helps avoid both copying every ancient rule directly and dismissing the entire collection without examining it.

Poetry communicates through images, paired lines, and feeling. When a psalm depicts hills clapping their hands, the image expresses creation's praise rather than describing a geological event. The claim belongs to the comparison the poem makes. Reading it as imagery takes its meaning seriously.

Wisdom sayings offer another kind of guidance. Proverbs often describes patterns in the way life works, giving readers reasons to act wisely. A general observation does not become a guarantee that the same outcome will occur in every case. That distinction matters when a reader tries to turn a proverb into a promise.

### Prophecy and vision
Prophetic speech often introduces warnings and promises as the LORD's word to a particular audience. The prophets name present injustice, announce judgment, and speak of hope beyond it. Prediction belongs within that work, but it does not replace attention to the people and circumstances being addressed.

Apocalyptic writing develops its message through visions. Daniel 7–12 and Revelation use beasts, numbers, and cosmic imagery to portray power, evil, and God's ultimate victory. The symbolism makes a claim about reality through images. Matching every image to a current news event without examining its original setting can obscure the message the first audience received.

### Parables, allegory, letters, and Gospels
A parable presents a story in order to make a point. When Jesus begins with a man who had two sons, the point does not depend on identifying those sons as historical people. The story's meaning is what the listener is asked to consider.

Allegory assigns representational meanings to details. Paul explicitly uses the term in Galatians 4:24, but that does not authorize readers to turn every story into a set of hidden symbols. Look for evidence in the text before assigning that kind of meaning.

Letters address real recipients and their questions or conflicts. Their teaching belongs to an argument made in that situation. The Gospels, in turn, present Jesus' life in the form of ancient biography, selecting and arranging material to communicate its significance. They are not modern day-by-day chronicles. These differences explain why audience, sequence, and purpose deserve attention in each form.

- “The mountains skipped like rams” (Psalm 114:4) → Poetry

### Where Christians read differently
Some passages do not receive the same classification from every serious Christian reader. Genesis 1–11 is understood as detailed literal history, as history told through stylized and symbolic forms, or as theological story using ancient literary forms. Each position makes a different judgment about how the text communicates its claims.

Jonah is read as history, as a historically based story shaped through satire, or as a prophetic story resembling a parable. Its question about God's mercy remains central under each reading. Job is likewise read as an account of a historical person or as a wisdom debate developed around a story.

Readers also disagree about Daniel's date, locating it in the sixth century BCE or understanding its visions as compiled in the second century BCE. Revelation receives readings focused on future events, recurring patterns across history, the first century, or combinations of those approaches. Recognizing these disagreements gives you questions to investigate; listing the options does not decide which is best supported.

### Not literal never means not true
Reading language as nonliteral does not, by itself, dismiss its truth. A parable can teach through a story, a psalm through an image, and a vision through a symbol. The task is to explain what the passage communicates in that form.

It would be equally mistaken to call every difficult passage symbolic without giving reasons. Where Christians disagree, Canonical Shelf presents the readings alongside one another and leaves the judgment with you. You can examine the evidence for each without treating a literary label as a shortcut to the conclusion.

### Glossary

**Historical narrative.** Historical narrative presents events as having happened and selects and arranges them to explain their significance. Recognizing narrative helps identify its claim, although it does not independently settle every question about the events.

**Parable.** A parable is a story told to communicate a point. Jesus' parables are not presented as reports about identifiable people; their truth concerns the meaning the story teaches.

**Allegory.** In allegory, elements of a story represent other subjects. Paul explicitly uses allegorical interpretation in Galatians 4. That example does not mean every biblical story should have a hidden meaning assigned to each detail.

**Apocalyptic.** Apocalyptic writing uses revelatory visions and symbols to address the reality of power, suffering, judgment, and hope. Daniel and Revelation contain this form, whose images need to be read in their literary and historical setting.

**Genre.** Genre means a kind of writing. Its conventions help readers recognize what a passage is doing and what kind of claim it makes, whether through narrative, command, imagery, or another form.

### Additional questions

#### If Genesis 1 is not literal, is it made up?

No. Christians who read Genesis 1 as stylized or theological still read it as true: it claims that God made the world good and gave people dignity and responsibility. The disagreement is about what kind of account it is, not whether it tells the truth.

#### Did Jesus think the parables were real events?

Parables were a known teaching form in Jesus’ world. Their power comes from their point, which is why they begin like stories rather than reports.

#### Who decides which reading is right?

You do, with evidence. Canonical Shelf states its own positions where it has them, shows the strongest alternatives fairly, and never scores agreement.

## Find your way: references, canon, and translation

Lesson ID: library

**Objective:** Read a Bible reference and distinguish canon, translation, and genre.

**Primary reading:** Luke 1:1–4

### Find your way
A Bible reference tells you where to find a passage. Canon tells you which writings belong to the collection you are using, and translation tells you how their language has been rendered. Distinguishing these questions helps explain why two Bibles may look different without either being fabricated.

The collection contains writings composed and preserved across many centuries. The Old Testament contains Israel's Scriptures; the New Testament includes four Gospels, Acts, letters, and Revelation. Christianity emerged within Judaism, so Israel's Scriptures should not be read as a failed preliminary version of Christianity. With that relationship in view, we can turn the earlier lessons on shelves and literary forms into a practical habit: locate a passage, identify the edition, and read the surrounding context.

### Read the passage with the question in view

### Read a reference like an address
Luke 1:1–4 identifies the book of Luke, chapter 1, and verses 1 through 4. Find those parts in that order. Chapter and verse numbers make a passage easier to locate, but their divisions do not take the place of the story or argument the writer composed. Opening the surrounding paragraphs helps you read a verse within that flow.

The same address can appear in several styles. Luke 1:1–4 uses the full book name; Lk 1:1–4 abbreviates it. Some British and academic writing uses Luke 1.1–4, placing a period between chapter and verse. Each version points to the same four verses. Learning the notation keeps a difference in style from becoming a difference in what you think is being cited.

### Put the kinds of writing to work
Psalm 23 portrays the LORD as a shepherd and places the speaker in a dark valley. Both belong to its account of trust. Reading the poem well means explaining how its images communicate God's care while danger remains present.

The earlier lesson introduced kinds of writing; here we will practice with one poem and compare it with narrative, commands, and a vision. A recipe, a news report, and a love poem can communicate truth through different conventions, just as biblical stories, laws, poems, proverbs, letters, and visions do. ### The shepherd and the host

The poem therefore gives language for confidence and dependence. It does not give readers a formula for predicting every outcome. Reading the whole passage keeps a statement about God's care connected with the situation in which that care is being confessed.

## Rupture, mortality, and the tree of life

Lesson ID: sin-mortality

**Objective:** Trace the consequences of sin while distinguishing mortality from inherited personal guilt.

**Primary reading:** Genesis 3:8–24

### Rupture, mortality, and the tree of life
Genesis 3 describes hiding, blame, consequences, and the closed way to the tree of life. Christians have interpreted this rupture in several different ways, so Canonical Shelf's position is stated before the details are examined.

**Canonical Shelf's own position.** The first human sin brought mortality into the human condition. Humanity inherits its consequences, including mortality and a damaged condition, but does not inherit the personal guilt of those who acted before us. Within that condition, each person eventually sins through choices of their own and is responsible for those choices.

Many Western Christian traditions, particularly those influenced by Augustine, also teach inherited guilt. That account belongs to the later interpretation of the passage rather than being something this lesson settles from Genesis alone. Keeping these positions distinct lets you examine their claims while reading how the story depicts the rupture.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Genesis 3:8–24 (BSB).

### Hiding and blame
The humans now respond to God with hiding and fear. When asked about their actions, they point toward others. These responses depict a rupture in trust and accountability before the story turns to toil and death.

Sin is consequently presented as more than breaking a detached rule. The choice affects how the humans relate to God and to one another. The earlier picture of trust helps explain why their fear and blame matter within the account.

### Consequences, not commands
The consequences reach through shared life: painful labor, strained relationships, and return to dust. Read these descriptions as an account of damage rather than instructions to create it. The story's depiction of suffering does not make suffering an ideal readers should preserve.

The same distinction applies to domination. Reporting a broken relationship does not command later people to reproduce it. Reading what the text describes and what it prescribes as separate questions prevents a consequence of rupture from being mistaken for a moral requirement.
