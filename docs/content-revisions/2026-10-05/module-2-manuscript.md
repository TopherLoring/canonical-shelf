## Exodus: hearing suffering and answering a call

Lesson ID: exodus-call

**Objective:** Explain how the call of Moses connects divine concern with human participation.

**Primary reading:** Exodus 3:7–15

### Exodus: hearing suffering and answering a call
Module 2 begins with Exodus, the story of deliverance that becomes central to Israel’s identity. We begin at Moses’ call because it connects God’s concern for suffering people with a human being’s participation in their rescue. God first speaks of seeing, hearing, and knowing the people’s affliction. Oppression means the use of power to constrain or harm others; here its meaning comes into focus through the experience of an enslaved community. Their suffering receives attention in its own right, rather than appearing as an inconvenience for the rulers who benefit from it.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Exodus 3:7–15 (BSB).

As you read, follow God’s verbs before you look for Moses’ assignment: God has seen, heard, known, and come down. That order places the commission within an announced response to suffering. Then listen to Moses ask who he is to undertake the task and what name he should give the people. His questions help us see the relationship between the purpose of the call and the uncertainty of the person receiving it.

### Called with questions
Moses receives a commission, a task entrusted to him for a stated purpose, and questions his ability to carry it out. The story keeps both elements together. God initiates the response to oppression while calling Moses to take part in it. Divine action and human participation therefore do not compete for a place in the narrative. Moses’ hesitation also remains visible: the significance of the task does not mean that the person undertaking it has become certain or no longer needs support.

### Presence and the divine name
God answers Moses’ concern about adequacy with an assurance of presence. The answer directs attention from Moses’ qualifications to the relationship in which he will act. God’s self-identification also connects the encounter with the ancestors, so the commission belongs within Israel’s continuing story. Read the divine name reverently and attend to translation notes. Its linguistic and theological significance cannot be exhausted by treating a single English rendering as a complete explanation.

Christians can read Exodus as forming concern for people under oppression. Applying that concern responsibly requires identifying the actual harm and listening to the people experiencing it before deciding how to participate. The story begins with attention to suffering, so an application that ignores those people’s experience loses the very connection it claims to make. A present conflict does not become a direct reenactment of Exodus merely because someone gives it that name. Liberation language needs evidence and respect for the agency of those being helped.

### What you now know
The call of Moses joins attention to an enslaved people’s suffering with the commission of a hesitant participant. God’s initiative gives the task its purpose, and Moses’ questions show that participation can include uncertainty. The assurance he receives is presence rather than a declaration that his own adequacy has become beyond question. Christians differ over how directly this story should shape modern politics. Canonical Shelf reads it as forming concern for people under oppression while requiring an argued connection to any present situation. Carry this question into the next lessons: what does deliverance require after people escape?

**Liberation.** Liberation is release from bondage or domination. In Exodus, rescue begins a continuing story of how a freed people will live.

**Commission.** A commission is a task entrusted to someone for a purpose. Moses receives a role in the response to Israel’s suffering even while questioning his adequacy.

## Passover: liberation remembered at a table

Lesson ID: c2-passover

**Objective:** Explain how Passover connects deliverance from Egypt with ritual memory and communal identity.

**Primary reading:** Exodus 12:1–14

### Passover: liberation remembered at a table
Moses’ call began a response to slavery; Exodus 12 brings us to the night of departure. Passover names the festival that remembers Israel’s deliverance from Egypt, and this lesson asks how a meal can preserve that memory as part of a community’s identity. Exodus places the meal within the final confrontation with Pharaoh. Danger, departure, and rescue give the instructions their meaning. Later generations are told to observe the festival because this night is formative for the people they understand themselves to be. The meal belongs to that liberation story before later readers develop its imagery.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Exodus 12:1–14 (BSB).

Read the passage with a household in view. Notice the lamb, the marked doorposts, and the clothing worn in readiness for travel. Follow those immediate instructions through to the command that the day be kept as a festival for future generations. The passage connects preparations for a particular night with an enduring practice of remembrance.

### The first Passover
At the first Passover, households prepare a lamb, mark their doors, and eat ready to leave. These actions are meaningful because departure is imminent; they are not simply directions for preparing a religious meal. The lasting observance preserves the memory of that setting. Begin with what the details do within Israel’s story before interpreting them through later Jewish or Christian uses. Otherwise, later symbolism can replace the event the passage asks its first audience to remember.

### A practice of memory
A memorial is a practice through which a community keeps a formative event present in its identity. Passover does this by giving later generations a recurring participation in Israel’s received story. Remembering is consequently more than recalling information about an earlier event. Exodus language can return in later biblical discussions of rescue, covenant, worship, and belonging because the deliverance provides a shared point of reference. The meal makes that memory a communal practice rather than leaving it as an isolated history lesson.

### Later readings of Passover
The New Testament places important traditions about Jesus within Passover settings and draws on Passover imagery. Module 3 will examine those connections, including distinctions that a first introduction cannot settle. Christian interpretation is a later use of an already meaningful Jewish festival. It should therefore explain its connection to Exodus while preserving Passover’s Jewish meaning, rather than treating the earlier story as a code whose only significance lies in later events.

### What you now know
Passover connects deliverance with ritual memory: a household meal eaten in readiness becomes an observance through which later generations remember their place in Israel’s story. This explains why the instructions combine immediate action with a lasting command. Jewish tradition continues to keep Passover, and the New Testament gives the festival an important place in the story of Jesus. Christians differ in how they draw the relationship between these settings. Canonical Shelf insists that those interpretations preserve the festival’s Jewish meaning. As you move forward, ask what a shared ritual of memory enables a community to do that a history lesson alone does not.

### Glossary

**Passover.** Passover is the biblical festival remembering Israel’s deliverance from Egypt. Its shared meal connects later generations with that formative story.

**Unleavened bread.** Unleavened bread is bread made without leaven. Exodus connects it with the urgency of leaving Egypt.

**Memorial.** A memorial is a practice that keeps a formative event present in a community’s identity. Passover gives remembrance a recurring communal form.

### Additional questions

#### Why eat in readiness?

Exodus depicts the meal in the setting of imminent departure. The posture reinforces that this is a liberation narrative, not simply a festival recipe.

#### Passover and Christianity

Course 4 will distinguish Exodus itself, Gospel Passover settings, the Last Supper traditions and later Christian interpretation rather than treating them as identical.

### Deeper reading note

Biblical Passover practice develops across texts and periods. Exodus 12, Deuteronomy 16, later reform narratives, Second Temple practice, and later Jewish tradition should not be flattened into one timeless ritual description.

## Sea and wilderness: freedom becomes dependence

Lesson ID: c2-sea-wilderness

**Objective:** Trace the movement from escape through the sea into wilderness dependence rather than treating Exodus as complete at departure.

**Primary reading:** Exodus 14:10–31

### Sea and wilderness: freedom becomes dependence
The departure from Egypt begins freedom, but it does not complete the Exodus story. At the sea, the people fear that escape has failed and that Egyptian domination will return. This lesson follows rescue through that danger and then into the wilderness, where the community continues to need provision and formation. The narrative presents deliverance as a movement that must be traced, rather than a political announcement after which every problem disappears. Following the people beyond departure shows why freedom and the ability to live well in freedom are related but distinct concerns.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Exodus 14:10–31 (BSB).

Follow the scene in order: fear, the instruction to stand firm, the opening of a path through the water, and the people’s response of seeing and trusting. Then attend to the thirst that appears in the chapters after the crossing. The placement of that need keeps rescue and continuing vulnerability together in the story.

### Wilderness: daily dependence
The wilderness is the unsettled region in which the rescued people face daily needs. Its stories return to food, water, complaint, fear, trust, and leadership because leaving slavery has not removed dependence. Manna, described as God’s provision of food, gives that dependence a daily form. It is not a stockpile that ends the need to trust for tomorrow. The repeated needs explain why the wilderness becomes a place of formation as well as a stage in a journey.

### Freedom is not instant maturity
Liberation does not instantly supply all the habits, institutions, memory, and trust needed for a community’s future. Exodus makes this visible by continuing to narrate vulnerability and complaint after the sea crossing. The people are freed from an oppressive system, yet their shared life still needs to develop. This is why the wilderness prevents a romantic account of liberation: rescue matters profoundly, but it is not identical with immediate maturity or the disappearance of all future difficulty.

### Echoes in later Scripture
Later Scripture repeatedly draws on the sea and wilderness stories. To understand those echoes, first learn the earlier movement they recall: danger leads to rescue, and rescue is followed by testing, provision, failure, and continuing presence. Later uses can emphasize different parts of that movement. Knowing the source narrative prevents the word wilderness from becoming a fixed label with the same meaning in every later passage.

### What you now know
Exodus carries the people through danger into rescue and then into a life of continuing dependence. Daily provision, testing, complaint, and presence show how the wilderness forms a freed community. Freedom from domination has therefore not made the people instantly mature. The historical details of the crossing and journey are debated; the narrative movement remains the focus of this reading, and later Scripture repeatedly returns to it. Keep both the rescue and the continuing vulnerability in view. The question to carry forward is what a freed community needs in order to live well together.

### Glossary

**Wilderness.** The wilderness is an unsettled region that becomes a setting of testing, provision, and formation in the Exodus traditions. Different biblical texts emphasize different aspects of that experience.

**Manna.** Manna is the food described as God’s provision for Israel in the wilderness. The narrative associates it with daily dependence.

**Deliverance.** Deliverance is rescue from danger or domination. The sea crossing belongs within Israel’s release from Egyptian bondage.

### Additional questions

#### Why wilderness matters later

Prophets, Psalms, Gospels, Paul, and Revelation reuse wilderness imagery. Course 5 will study how later texts reuse earlier stories without making every reuse identical.

### Deeper reading note

Different biblical texts remember the wilderness with different emphases—rebellion, dependence, courtship, judgment, provision. A theme should preserve those differences instead of turning “wilderness” into one fixed symbol.

## Promise, liberation, and covenant

Lesson ID: covenant

**Objective:** Trace how promise and liberation lead to covenant responsibilities and concern for neighbors.

**Primary reading:** Genesis 12:1–3

### Promise, liberation, and covenant
God’s call to Abram connects a particular household with blessing that reaches beyond it. This lesson follows that promise through liberation and into covenant responsibility. A covenant is a committed relationship that involves promises and responsibilities; it helps us understand how belonging can shape a shared way of life. Reading the promise merely as a reward for exceptional people overlooks its outward direction. The call begins a long story in which trust, failure, conflict, and continuing relationship all have a place. Blessing beyond Abram’s family gives the beginning its wider purpose.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Genesis 12:1–3 (BSB).

Read Genesis 12:1–3 at the beginning of this narrative movement. Listen especially for blessing and for the people beyond Abram’s own household who come within the promise’s horizon. That outward direction will help connect the promise with the later stories of liberation and communal responsibility.

### From promise to liberation
Exodus next portrays God attending to the suffering of an enslaved people and calling Moses into their liberation. Moses’ questions and limitations are kept within the account; neither his perfection nor an already perfected Israelite society explains the rescue. The narrative moves from oppression toward freedom before describing the responsibilities of communal life. This order matters because it prevents us from treating deliverance as a payment that Israel has already earned through flawless conduct.

### A communal vocation at Sinai
At Sinai, covenant gives Israel a communal vocation, a calling that concerns the people’s shared life. Instructions about worship and social conduct belong together within that relationship. Leviticus 19 makes the connection concrete through produce left for vulnerable people, honesty in dealings, justice, and love of neighbor. Holiness therefore concerns the treatment of others as well as worship. Those instructions explain how belonging to a covenant community carries responsibilities that reach into ordinary life.

Promise, liberation, and covenant form a connected movement. ## Sinai: a liberated people enters covenant

Lesson ID: c2-sinai-covenant

**Objective:** Connect Sinai covenant with the Exodus that precedes it and distinguish covenant identity from earning liberation.

**Primary reading:** Exodus 19:1–8

### Sinai: a liberated people enters covenant
Israel reaches Sinai as a people already brought out of Egypt. This lesson reads the moment before the commandments and asks how that earlier rescue gives covenant responsibility its setting. God first recalls the deliverance and then describes the relationship into which Israel is called. The sequence explains why the instructions are not the price paid for release from slavery. They describe life within a relationship that has already begun, rather than a technique by which an enslaved people earns its freedom.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Exodus 19:1–8 (BSB).

Notice the order of God’s speech. The image of being carried on eagles’ wings recalls what God has already done; the invitation that follows concerns what Israel is to become. Keep that movement visible as you read the people’s response.

### Treasured possession and priestly vocation
Covenant brings together promise, relationship, identity, and obligation. Exodus 19 describes Israel as a treasured possession and gives the people a priestly vocation and a call to holiness. These terms concern a people’s place and responsibilities within a larger world. A vocation is a calling to a task, so the language should not be converted into a license for moral superiority. The identity named here carries a responsibility rather than establishing that every Israelite action is good.

Torah means instruction, and the teaching that follows Sinai addresses several dimensions of shared life. Sinai follows rescue, and covenant responsibilities describe how the rescued community is to live. ## Golden calf: covenant can be broken and renewed

Lesson ID: c2-golden-calf

**Objective:** Explain the golden-calf episode as covenant rupture within the Sinai story and identify the roles of judgment, intercession, and renewal.

**Primary reading:** Exodus 32:1–14

### Golden calf: covenant can be broken and renewed
The golden-calf episode interrupts the Sinai material soon after Israel receives covenant instruction. Its placement shows that instruction has not produced a community incapable of failure. Anxiety over Moses’ absence leads into the making of an image and a crisis of worship and loyalty. This lesson follows that crisis through judgment, intercession, and renewal. Keeping these movements together allows us to understand covenant rupture as a serious failure within an existing relationship, rather than imagining either an idealized people or a relationship that simply disappears.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Exodus 32:1–14 (BSB).

Notice how quickly the people act when Moses is delayed, and attend to their anxiety about his absence. Then follow Moses’ appeal on their behalf. The movement from the image to the appeal helps locate the episode within the wider covenant story.

### Intercession inside the relationship
Intercession means appealing on behalf of other people. Moses intercedes by recalling God’s relationship with Israel and the promises associated with the ancestors. His appeal matters because judgment is being narrated within that continuing relationship. The story does not treat Israel’s failure as evidence that the relationship and earlier promises never existed. Moses’ argument holds those commitments before God while taking the crisis seriously.

### Consequence and renewal
The following chapters describe consequences and covenant renewal. Renewal means that a damaged relationship is restored or reaffirmed; it does not mean the damage was imaginary. Reading consequence and restoration together preserves accountability as well as mercy. Biblical covenant life can contain actual failure and judgment while still allowing a restored path forward. Neither element cancels the other.

The golden-calf narrative holds failure, judgment, intercession, consequence, mercy, and renewal within covenant life. ### Additional questions

A modern application needs an argued analogy. Calling money, politics, work, or another concern an “idol” may be rhetorically powerful, but the connection should be explained rather than assumed.

### Deeper reading note

Exodus 32–34 is a literary unit worth reading together. A lesson on “the golden calf” that stops at the image can miss Moses’ intercession, divine self-description, consequences, and covenant renewal.

## Torah: holiness in everyday relationships

Lesson ID: torah-neighbor

**Objective:** Connect covenant instruction with concrete care while avoiding mechanical transfer of ancient law.

**Primary reading:** Leviticus 19:9–18

### Torah: holiness in everyday relationships
Leviticus 19 brings covenant instruction into ordinary relationships. Its selected laws address farming, wages, truthful dealing, impartial judgment, and the treatment of neighbors. This lesson asks how those concrete concerns belong to holiness and how an ancient instruction can be applied responsibly. Economic decisions and speech are part of the community’s response to God, because they affect the people with whom covenant life is shared. Holiness therefore cannot be confined to ritual activity while the rest of life is treated as unrelated.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Leviticus 19:9–18 (BSB).

Read Leviticus 19:9–18 with its situations in view: a field at harvest, a worker awaiting payment, a courtroom, and a grudge between neighbors. Notice how the repeated declaration “I am the LORD” places these varied responsibilities within the same covenant setting.

### Leaving the edges of the field
Gleaning means gathering produce left after the main harvest. The instructions protect access to food for poor people and foreigners by requiring that some produce remain available. They operate within a particular agricultural arrangement, so responsible present application must first understand that arrangement. Then it can identify the concern for access and protection and explain how a proposed response expresses it. The concern is not understood fully by copying a rule without considering the world in which it works.
