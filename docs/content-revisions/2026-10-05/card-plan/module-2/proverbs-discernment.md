## Proverbs: wisdom is more than a slogan

### 1 of 9 · 532 / 637

Proverbs 26:4–5 places two sayings about answering a fool side by side. One directs against answering and the other directs an answer. This lesson asks why their reasons make attention to circumstances necessary. Reading either saying as an absolute rule would remove the pressure created by their adjacency. Proverbs cultivates discernment, careful judgment about a situation and an appropriate response, by requiring the reader to identify the danger each saying addresses. Wisdom therefore involves more than retrieving a slogan.

## Two dangers, two sayings

### 3 of 9 · 414 / 637

The first warning concerns becoming like the person being answered. The second concerns leaving that person wise in their own eyes. Both evaluate the effect of a response, but they identify different effects. The choice therefore requires asking whether an answer would join the person’s folly or whether silence would leave error unchallenged. Neither reason disappears simply because the other is also important.

## Wisdom as judgment

### 5 of 9 · 388 / 637

A wise decision considers audience, purpose, and likely consequences. Silence may avoid an unproductive quarrel, while calm correction may protect people from being misled. Those possibilities explain why either response can be appropriate without becoming the automatic response to every situation. Wisdom involves evaluating the circumstances rather than declaring one verse irrelevant.

## Using the word “fool” carefully

### 6 of 9 · 380 / 637

Fool in wisdom writing should not become a label that dismisses anyone who disagrees. Test the disputed claim and the effect of the response you propose. A reply can correct a false statement and acknowledge uncertainty while refusing contempt. That approach keeps the concern with wise conduct from becoming an excuse for the very kind of exchange the first saying warns against.

## What you now know

### 8 of 9 · 552 / 637

These paired sayings teach judgment by naming two dangers: being drawn into folly and leaving folly unchallenged. The reader must consider which consequence the situation presents. Proverbs offers discernment rather than a formula that guarantees outcomes, and its language about a fool does not license dismissal of disagreement. Readers broadly understand proverbs as describing patterns rather than certifying what will happen in every case. The next lesson asks what occurs when suffering breaks an expected pattern and confident explanations fail.
