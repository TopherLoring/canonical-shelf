## Micah: justice, mercy, and humility

### 1 of 9 · 500 / 637

Micah speaks to Judah in the same century as Amos and is a contemporary of Isaiah. This lesson follows the question-and-answer movement of Micah 6:6–8. The questions consider what someone might bring before God, then escalate toward extreme offerings. They create a problem rather than endorse every possibility mentioned. The answer changes the direction from the quantity of an offering toward the way a person lives with God and others. Following that change explains the passage’s ethical demand.

## Justice, mercy, and a humble walk

### 3 of 9 · 426 / 637

The answer calls for doing justice, loving mercy, and walking humbly with God. These are relational actions and dispositions. Mercy is compassion expressed in the treatment of others, and humility recognizes limits and accountability. Together they challenge the premise that enough religious offering can bypass responsibility for one’s conduct. The passage gives a way of living rather than a price list for approaching God.

## Humility for the reader too

### 5 of 9 · 362 / 637

Humility also applies to the reader who uses this text. Prophetic application does not grant infallible insight into another person’s motives. We need honest evidence, attention to people affected, and willingness to examine our own conduct. These requirements follow from the very accountability the passage names, rather than exempting the interpreter from it.

## A demand, not a price

### 6 of 9 · 343 / 637

Micah’s demand can guide practical action without becoming a technique for purchasing salvation. Within this course, grace is God’s gift and ethical action is a response to it. Keeping that distinction preserves both the gift and the demand: action does not earn grace, yet justice, mercy, and humility still make claims on how a person lives.

## What you now know

### 8 of 9 · 557 / 637

The passage redirects an escalating inquiry about offerings toward justice, mercy, and a humble walk with God. The response concerns life in relationship rather than the price of religious success, and humility remains relevant to how readers judge others. Canonical Shelf’s Statement of Faith holds that grace is given and ethical action responds to it rather than earns it. Micah’s demand retains its force within that understanding.
