## Exile: history heard alongside grief

### 1 of 9 · 501 / 637

Psalm 137 voices displacement beside the rivers of Babylon through grief and memory of Zion, a name associated with Jerusalem and its religious significance. This lesson reads that lament alongside history while distinguishing an expression of pain from a command to imitate every action it imagines. Exile involves more than movement on a map: worship, belonging, familiar institutions, and a shared story are also affected. The poem allows us to hear what those losses mean to a displaced community.

## “Sing us one of the songs of Zion”

### 3 of 9 · 364 / 637

The captors’ demand for songs places the community’s grief under pressure from others. Its question about singing a sacred song in a foreign land belongs to that emotional and communal situation. It should not be lifted out as a universal rule forbidding worship outside Jerusalem. Attend to who demands the song and what the demand does to those who are grieving.

## Memory as loyalty

### 5 of 9 · 353 / 637

The vow to remember Jerusalem expresses loyalty amid loss. Read it beside the destruction in 2 Kings 25 to see what poetry contributes to the account: the experience of memory and grief within catastrophe. A chronology locates events, while lament voices their human significance. Both can contribute evidence without providing the same kind of account.

## The disturbing ending

### 6 of 9 · 407 / 637

The full psalm ends in disturbing language of vengeance. Do not hide those verses, and do not assume that making pain and anger speakable makes every imagined action ethically acceptable. Canonical Shelf reads Christian application through Jesus’ teaching and the dignity of other people. The difficulty remains visible when the cry is heard as lament rather than turned into instruction to repeat violence.

## What you now know

### 8 of 9 · 515 / 637

Psalm 137 gives communal loss a voice through setting, the demand for songs, and a pledge of remembrance. Its final cry for terrible vengeance also belongs to the poem; listening to grief does not require treating that cry as a command. Christian traditions differ over how to pray or sing such psalms while reading them alongside Jesus’ teaching about enemies. Canonical Shelf keeps both the anger and the ethical question visible. The last unit now turns toward hope and asks what the prophets promise after loss.
