## Tabernacle: sacred space on the move

### 1 of 8 · 552 / 637

The tabernacle is the portable sanctuary described in Exodus. This lesson turns from covenant instruction to the ordered sacred space in which Israel’s worship takes place. Exodus devotes substantial attention to it because its stated purpose concerns God dwelling among the people brought out of Egypt. The construction material therefore belongs within the deliverance story rather than functioning as an unrelated architectural appendix. The sanctuary is designed for a people still on the move, so presence and movement must be understood together.

## Graded space: courtyard, Holy Place, Most Holy Place

### 3 of 8 · 501 / 637

The spaces become increasingly restricted as one moves inward. The outer courtyard leads to the tent’s Holy Place, its first chamber, and then to the Most Holy Place, also called the Holy of Holies. The innermost space is associated with the Ark. Furnishings and priestly actions belong in particular zones; their placement shows that presence, holiness, access, and worship are related. Learning those relationships is more useful than remembering a list of objects without knowing where they belong.

## A sanctuary on the move

### 5 of 8 · 394 / 637

The tabernacle travels with Israel because it is portable. In this stage of the story, divine presence is therefore not tied to a permanent royal capital. The later Jerusalem temple will take up and transform many of the sanctuary’s spatial and ritual patterns. Beginning with the traveling sanctuary helps explain the continuity between tent and temple without making their settings identical.

## What you now know

### 8 of 8 · 564 / 637

The tabernacle’s stated purpose is God dwelling among Israel. Its graded spaces locate worship and access in relation to holiness, and its portability associates presence with a traveling people. Scholars debate the relationship of these descriptions to later temple practice and the period in which they took their present form. Those debates should be distinguished from the spatial and ritual relationships taught by the text itself. Carry that distinction forward as the next lesson enters the innermost room: what object belongs there, and why does it matter?
