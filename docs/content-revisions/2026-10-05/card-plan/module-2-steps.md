### Exodus: hearing suffering and answering a call

#### 1 of 10 · 578 / 637

Module 2 begins with Exodus, the story of deliverance that becomes central to Israel’s identity. We begin at Moses’ call because it connects God’s concern for suffering people with a human being’s participation in their rescue. God first speaks of seeing, hearing, and knowing the people’s affliction. Oppression means the use of power to constrain or harm others; here its meaning comes into focus through the experience of an enslaved community. ### Passover: liberation remembered at a table

#### 1 of 9 · 606 / 637

Moses’ call began a response to slavery; Exodus 12 brings us to the night of departure. Passover names the festival that remembers Israel’s deliverance from Egypt, and this lesson asks how a meal can preserve that memory as part of a community’s identity. Exodus places the meal within the final confrontation with Pharaoh. Danger, departure, and rescue give the instructions their meaning. Later generations are told to observe the festival because this night is formative for the people they understand themselves to be. The meal belongs to that liberation story before later readers develop its imagery.

### Sea and wilderness: freedom becomes dependence

#### 1 of 8 · 592 / 637

The departure from Egypt begins freedom, but it does not complete the Exodus story. At the sea, the people fear that escape has failed and that Egyptian domination will return. The narrative presents deliverance as a movement that must be traced, rather than a political announcement after which every problem disappears. Following the people beyond departure shows why freedom and the ability to live well in freedom are related but distinct concerns.

### Jeremiah: hope addressed to a displaced community

#### 1 of 7 · 471 / 637

Jeremiah’s letter addresses a community living in exile. Building, planting, forming households, and seeking the city’s welfare are instructions for sustained life amid displacement. Their presence shows that the audience is not promised an immediate disappearance of its circumstances. This lesson reads the familiar promise of future and hope within that communal audience and its extended time horizon, so the kind of hope offered is understood from the whole address.

### Locate the claim in context

#### 3 of 7 · 572 / 637

The warning about misleading voices belongs alongside the promise. Reassurance does not become trustworthy simply because it sounds religious; the letter directs the people toward practices they can sustain while waiting. Its future and hope belong within this communal address and its stated horizon. They do not guarantee instant financial success or a preferred career for each present reader. Context does not remove the hope. It identifies a hope that can accompany a people through a prolonged period rather than certifying the immediate outcome an individual wants.
