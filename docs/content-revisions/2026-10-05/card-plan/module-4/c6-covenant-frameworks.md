## Covenant frameworks: continuity without erasing Israel

### 1 of 7 · 525 / 637

Earlier study traced Noahic, Abrahamic, Sinai, Davidic, and new-covenant trajectories in biblical history. Christian theology then asks how those covenants relate and how the Church relates to Israel. A framework organizes answers to those questions, but different frameworks emphasize different relationships. Our objective is to compare them while retaining the texts and rejecting Gentile superiority. Romans 11 is consequential here because its olive-tree image warns Gentile believers against boasting over the branches.

## Locate the claim in context

### 3 of 7 · 555 / 637

Covenant theology is a family of systems that commonly emphasizes continuity across God's covenantal purposes. It often organizes Scripture through theological covenants such as works and grace, with important variation among Reformed and other traditions. Dispensationalism is another family, generally making sharper distinctions between administrations in biblical history and often maintaining a distinctive future role for ethnic or national Israel. These broad descriptions orient a comparison rather than eliminating variation within either family.

Other traditions reject both systems or use different categories. Romans 9–11 remains central because Paul speaks about Gentile inclusion, Israel, divine faithfulness, warning against Gentile boasting, and unresolved mystery together. A framework that explains only one of those concerns still has to account for the others. Its organizing categories do not replace the connected argument of the text.

## Keep the setting visible

### 5 of 7 · 629 / 637

Supersessionism names a range of claims that the Church supersedes or replaces Israel, so the term requires definition before it can evaluate a particular position. A claim about fulfillment in Christ does not automatically prove that God abandoned Jewish people. Canonical Shelf rejects the idea that Christian belonging permits contempt for Jews or establishes such abandonment. A responsible framework must account for the actual texts and the ethical consequences of its language about Israel and Judaism. The warning against boasting constrains application even where Christians continue to disagree about the larger system.
