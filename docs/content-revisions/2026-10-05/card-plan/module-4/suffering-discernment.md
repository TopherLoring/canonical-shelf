## Suffering, miracles, and spiritual discernment

### 1 of 8 · 497 / 637

Luke 13 addresses the inference that victims of particular tragedies were worse sinners than others. Jesus challenges that judgment through examples of human violence and a falling tower. The passage does not supply a complete theory of suffering, but it directly resists this way of blaming victims. Our objective is to distinguish theological questions from unsupported conclusions about a person’s illness or loss. Care can begin before every question about God and suffering has been answered.

## Locate the claim in context

### 3 of 8 · 559 / 637

The guide distinguishes moral evil, or harm connected with human wrongdoing, from disease and disasters within a dynamic natural world. God's ability to bring good from suffering does not mean that every tragedy was desired or required. Love, freedom, lament, and responsible care remain part of the discussion. These distinctions keep a claim about possible healing from becoming an excuse for the harm itself.

Biblical miracle and deliverance accounts deserve attention to their narrative claims and purposes.

Christians differ over personal, symbolic, and systemic readings of spiritual evil. Those differences require interpretation; they do not authorize treating a modern illness as evidence of possession or an unhealed condition as evidence of inadequate faith. Suffering by itself does not establish such a spiritual diagnosis.

## Keep the setting visible

### 5 of 8 · 560 / 637

When someone suffers, begin with immediate needs, listening, and appropriate help. Leave room for prayer and unanswered questions without imposing an account of God's hidden intention. A spiritual label must not displace practical care. Theodicy is reasoning about God's goodness in relation to evil and suffering, and Luke's rejection of one inference does not resolve every question in that discussion. Our task is to state what the passage challenges and what remains uncertain, while offering care that does not depend on possessing a complete explanation.
