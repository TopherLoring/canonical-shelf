## Put understanding into practice

### 1 of 9 · 462 / 637

A parable is a story that invites an audience to recognize, question, or reconsider something. In Luke's conversation, a legal expert asks about eternal life and then asks who counts as a neighbor. Jesus answers with the story of an assaulted traveler left injured. The Samaritan becomes a neighbor by giving concrete care across a social boundary. Our task is to see how the story redirects the question of who deserves mercy toward the action of practicing it.

## Locate the claim in context

### 3 of 9 · 590 / 637

The story names a priest, a Levite, and a Samaritan. Giving the Samaritan this role unsettles expectations across a real social and religious boundary. The passage does not tell us every motive of the people who pass by, so a confident explanation of their reasons would add something the story has not supplied. We can examine the actions it describes without pretending to know those motives.

Mercy takes practical form as the Samaritan approaches the injured person, tends wounds, transports him, arranges continuing care, and pays for it.

## Keep the setting visible

### 5 of 9 · 524 / 637

Canonical Shelf teaches forgiveness and salvation as gifts of God's Love through Christ's sacrifice and resurrection. They are received rather than earned, not rewards for deeds, rituals, or prescribed words. Faith and repentance respond to the gift rather than paying for it. The parable nevertheless gives discipleship concrete ethical content: mercy involves action and continuing responsibility. Holding grace and conduct together prevents Christianity from becoming either a merit contest or words without consequences.
