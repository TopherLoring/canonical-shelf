## Locate the claim in context

### 3 of 10 · 608 / 637

Zacchaeus speaks about giving and repayment. Readers debate the statement's tense and implications, but its ethical concern is evident: money and the effects of wrongdoing matter in this encounter. A religious experience cannot be detached from the neighbors affected by one's conduct. Restitution means returning what was taken or compensating for a concrete loss where possible.

The guide treats repair as a response to grace. Acknowledging the people harmed makes repentance more credible, because wrongdoing has consequences beyond the offender's feelings.

### 4 of 10 · 619 / 637

Forgiveness, however, does not entitle an offender to demand immediate trust or unrestricted access to the person injured. Trust concerns whether someone can safely be relied upon; it need not be restored merely because forgiveness is being considered.

Canonical Shelf's own account of repentance has four parts: recognizing that an action contradicted God's loving purpose, taking responsibility without deflection or self-condemnation, apologizing, and genuinely intending a different outcome. Whether the later change succeeds is not what makes the repentance genuine.

## Keep the setting visible

### 6 of 10 · 472 / 637

Practical repair can include admission, restitution where possible, changed habits, and respect for boundaries that protect others. Different harms call for different responses, so an apology is not a universal remedy for every loss. Forgiveness, repaired material harm, and rebuilt trust are connected but distinct. The lesson establishes a theological relationship between grace and conduct; it does not promise that every damaged relationship can be restored on demand.
