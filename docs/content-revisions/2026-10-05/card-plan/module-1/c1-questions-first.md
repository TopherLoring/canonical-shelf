## Bring your questions

### 1 of 13 · 576 / 637

Adults often begin Bible study with questions already in mind. Why does suffering exist? Why did Jesus die? What is sin? Can Scripture be trusted after centuries of copying and translation? Why do Christians disagree about sexuality, women, hell, miracles, predestination, violence, other religions, and which commands still apply? These questions help explain why the curriculum exists.

You will meet them early, receive an initial map, and return to them as you learn the evidence and context needed to weigh stronger answers.

## Important is not the same as simple

### 3 of 13 · 491 / 637

A question can matter deeply and still require more than a short answer. One verse may belong to an ancient language, a literary genre, a covenant, a historical conflict, or a longer argument. Later Christians may also have used it in a doctrinal debate. An answer that ignores those layers can sound confident without explaining the passage well.

Acts describes hearers who receive a claim seriously and examine the Scriptures to evaluate it.

## How the path answers

### 6 of 13 · 633 / 637

The first part of the path gives you orientation and responsible first answers. You meet Christianity's central claim, the questions that worry new readers, the Bible as a library, and the story from creation to Abraham. That map lets you see where a question belongs before you have studied all the material needed to answer it.

The next two parts develop the biblical and historical setting. You read covenant and law, sacrifice and temple, wisdom, exile, and hope. Then you enter the Second Temple world and study Jesus, the cross and resurrection, Acts, Paul, and the early Church.

## Holding an answer lightly

### 9 of 13 · 556 / 637

A provisional answer is one you can use for now while remaining willing to change it. You may arrive at an initial understanding, study further, and revise your judgment when stronger evidence becomes available. Changing an answer for a reason is part of learning.

This does not require you to postpone every conclusion or assume that all answers are equally uncertain. Some claims are better supported than others. The point is to make your level of confidence fit the evidence you have learned to evaluate.

## What you now know

### 11 of 13 · 590 / 637

Your questions belong in the study from the beginning. The path introduces them early, provides an initial map, and returns to them as your knowledge of Scripture and its setting grows. An important question may need several kinds of evidence, which explains why the curriculum does not promise an immediate final answer to every concern.

Christians continue to disagree about many of these subjects. Later lessons explain the competing readings and their reasons, rather than assuming that disagreement makes every answer equally persuasive.
