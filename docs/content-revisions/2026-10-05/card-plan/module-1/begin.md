## Start with a person and a story

### 1 of 21 · 600 / 637

Christianity centers on Jesus of Nazareth: his life, his death, and the proclamation that he sacrificed himself for our sins and rose from his tomb three days later. The Canonical Shelf is built for you to study what that claim means before deciding what you believe about it.

Paul passed on a compact summary of this message to a community he knew. It is one of the earliest surviving written statements of the Christian proclamation, and it gives us a starting point for the Learning Path. The shape of the Bible, Christian practices, and the arguments Christians still have all connect with this central story.

## What the words mean

### 6 of 21 · 575 / 637

Three words help explain the proclamation Paul has just recalled: gospel, Christ, and resurrection. Knowing their meanings makes it easier to say what the passage claims without confusing a title, a message, and an event.

In the New Testament, gospel translates the Greek word euangelion, meaning good news or good announcement. In the ancient world, good-news language could announce a military victory or celebrate a ruler. Such announcements mattered because they concerned events understood to change people's shared world.

*Count: prose 523 + internal spaces 3 + paragraph allowance 49 + blocks 0 = 575.*

### 7 of 21 · 559 / 637

Paul applies this language to Jesus' death and resurrection, events Christians understand to change humanity's relationship with God. The announcement therefore reaches beyond a hearer's private feelings. The word later also became the name for the first four books of the New Testament that tell Jesus' story: Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John. The message and the books are related uses of the same term.

Christ comes from the Greek word christos, meaning anointed one. It corresponds to the Hebrew title Messiah.

*Count: prose 506 + internal spaces 4 + paragraph allowance 49 + blocks 0 = 559.*

### 8 of 21 · 623 / 637

Calling Jesus the Christ therefore places him within Israel's hopes for the one God would send; it is not a statement about his family name. That connection with Israel's story will become clearer as you read the rest of the library.

Resurrection means being raised from death to a new, bodily life. In this proclamation it does not mean returning as a ghost, resuming the same mortal life, or being reborn as another person. The distinction matters because Paul is announcing what Christians say happened to Jesus, rather than using death and life as interchangeable images.

## A message inside a letter

### 10 of 21 · 576 / 637

These words occur inside a letter to an existing community. Paul is addressing people who already know the message and who have questions and disagreements. He recalls the death, burial, resurrection, and appearances because those claims belong to the proclamation they received together.

The passage is consequently brief. It is not a complete biography of Jesus, and Paul is not trying to tell every episode of his life here. He assumes a shared story and recalls its central events for a particular purpose within the letter.

### 11 of 21 · 491 / 637

Reading the summary as a biography would leave us wondering why so much is missing; reading it as a reminder helps explain its form.

The same habit will help with other biblical passages. Ask who is speaking, whom they are addressing, and why they are saying this here. A passage written to particular people in particular circumstances can teach later readers, but understanding those circumstances comes before deciding how it applies to us.

## The community behind the letter

### 12 of 21 · 533 / 637

The people receiving the letter lived in Roman Corinth. Paul was writing to a community whose members shared meals and relationships, rather than addressing only isolated individuals. In that world, patronage linked resources and assistance with expectations of loyalty and public honor. A gift could also create an obligation.

That background helps us ask who held influence in the community and whose needs were overlooked. It does not tell us the private motive of every Corinthian.

### 13 of 21 · 518 / 637

We can use a historical setting to ask better questions without pretending that it supplies facts the passage never gives.

Read 1 Corinthians 11:17–22 alongside this lesson. Paul describes some people going hungry while others have plenty, and he rebukes the humiliation of those who have nothing. Those details support a criticism of the community's conduct. They also show why the gospel recalled in chapter 15 concerns shared life as well as what individuals believe.

## What you now know

### 18 of 21 · 539 / 637

You can now put Paul's proclamation into your own words: Christ died for sins, was buried, was raised, and appeared to witnesses. Gospel names the good news Paul passes on; Christ is a title meaning anointed one; resurrection names being raised to new, bodily life. Paul recalls this message to a real community whose shared conduct also matters to him.

The purpose of this first lesson is orientation. Begin with what the passage says before trying to settle every question about salvation.

*Count: prose 487 + internal spaces 3 + paragraph allowance 49 + blocks 0 = 539.*

### 19 of 21 · 541 / 637

Later lessons distinguish Christ's saving work, receiving grace through faith and repentance, and the life that follows in response to grace. Those are connected subjects, but this short proclamation is not a full explanation of each one.

Christians have long shared this center while debating how to explain it. What does dying for sins accomplish? What kind of body does resurrection involve? How should the appearances be understood? Later lessons take up those questions with more context.
