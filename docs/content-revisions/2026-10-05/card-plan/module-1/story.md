The larger story Christians trace through the Bible moves from creation and human estrangement through God's covenant with Israel, Israel's life and crises, Jesus' ministry, and the early Church to hope for renewed creation. This lesson provides the whole map before the following lessons examine its opening chapters more closely.

The map follows relationships and turning points, rather than the dates when books were written. The collection is arranged partly by literary group, so its shelf order is not a simple timeline.

## Covenant, exile, and the prophets

### 4 of 14 · 579 / 637

Covenant describes a binding relationship with commitments. God's dealings with Abraham, Israel at Sinai, and David give later biblical hopes their vocabulary. Reading a later promise without knowing those relationships can make it sound detached from the story that explains it.

Exile describes displacement from a homeland. The prophets address injustice, judgment, and hope within the concrete circumstances of Israel's history and its crises. Their words belong to those circumstances before later readers apply them elsewhere.

## Jesus announces God’s kingdom

### 6 of 14 · 536 / 637

Jesus announces God's kingdom. Here kingdom concerns God's reign and its realization, rather than simply a location people reach after death. The announcement connects what God is doing with a response from those who hear it.

Mark names repentance and faith. Repentance means turning or reorienting life toward God, so it involves more than shame. Within the larger map, Jesus' preaching brings Israel's hopes into the foreground and asks his hearers to respond to the reign he announces.

## Jesus within Judaism

### 9 of 14 · 502 / 637

Jesus and his earliest followers were Jewish. Their language about covenant, kingdom, and fulfillment belongs within Jewish Scripture and history. A Christian map of the Bible should help explain that connection, rather than removing Jesus from it.

Christian interpretations must not assign collective guilt for Jesus' death to Jewish people or assume that God abandoned them. Judaism remains a living tradition with its own readings of these Scriptures.

## What you now know

### 12 of 14 · 610 / 637

The larger Christian narrative moves from creation and estrangement through God's covenant with Israel, Israel's life and crises, Jesus and the kingdom, and the early Church to hope for what comes. Covenant names committed relationship; exile locates displacement and crisis; God's kingdom concerns his reign; repentance names a reorientation of life.

Christians broadly share this outline but differ about how fulfillment in Jesus relates to Israel's continuing covenant and what final hope involves. Canonical Shelf leaves creation's final physical state open.
