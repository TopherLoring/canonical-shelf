## Created for life and responsibility

### 1 of 14 · 608 / 637

Genesis opens with God bringing an ordered world into being and calling it good. Human beings belong within that creation and are described as bearing God's image. Canonical Shelf understands that language to ground every person's dignity and responsibility, rather than a ranking by power, usefulness, gender, or sexuality.

Human action matters because people share the world with other creatures, and their choices affect the life around them. The following lessons develop that responsibility through the garden, the rupture, and the care of a living world.

## Provision, work, and a boundary

### 4 of 14 · 547 / 637

The garden account places provision, work, and a boundary together. The human is put in the garden to cultivate and keep what God has provided. Receiving life in this setting includes responsibility for it.

Freedom is therefore exercised within relationships and obligations. A theological reading asks what the account says about dependence on God and trust, and how humans live within a world they did not make. These concerns give the next lesson its focus before chapter 3 describes the rupture.

## Distrust and lost access

### 5 of 14 · 595 / 637

Genesis 3 describes distrust, disobedience, damaged relationships, and expulsion from the garden. It ends with access to the tree of life closed. The guide emphasizes the loss of humanity's intended enduring life with God and the inheritance of mortality and a broken condition.

Christians disagree about whether people also inherit personal guilt. The later lesson states Canonical Shelf's position explicitly and distinguishes it from other accounts. This account of humanity does not require a claim that animals could not die before human sin.

## Ancient language and modern questions

### 7 of 14 · 531 / 637

Genesis speaks through ancient language and literary forms. The questions it asks about God, the world, and humanity are not formulated as modern accounts of biological mechanisms. A creation narrative can have theological and historical significance without functioning as a scientific timeline.

Canonical Shelf does not require young-earth creationism. Read the claims the passage makes and distinguish them from modern scientific questions it does not formulate in the same terms.

## A world worth caring for

### 10 of 14 · 619 / 637

Psalm 104 returns to the abundance and dependence of living creatures. Its attention supports treating creation as valuable rather than disposable. Creation care is a response to that value and to the responsibility introduced in Genesis.

A practical response may reduce harm, protect resources shared with neighbors, or ask who bears the cost of consumption. Those actions express responsibility for a world received as a gift. They do not purchase salvation. The later lesson examines how a reason to care can be translated into an action whose real effects are tested.

## What you now know

### 12 of 14 · 562 / 637

Genesis 1 introduces a good, ordered creation and humanity bearing God's image. In this guide, that image grounds every person's dignity and responsibility. The garden, the rupture, and creation care develop those concerns in the lessons that follow. The guide's position on inherited guilt is stated in the lesson on Genesis 3.

Christians broadly affirm creation's goodness and human dignity while disagreeing about what the image of God consists of. Canonical Shelf takes no definitive position on that question.
