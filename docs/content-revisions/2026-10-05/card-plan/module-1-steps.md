### What the words mean

#### 6 of 21 · 575 / 637

Three words help explain the proclamation Paul has just recalled: gospel, Christ, and resurrection. Knowing their meanings makes it easier to say what the passage claims without confusing a title, a message, and an event.

In the New Testament, gospel translates the Greek word euangelion, meaning good news or good announcement. In the ancient world, good-news language could announce a military victory or celebrate a ruler. Such announcements mattered because they concerned events understood to change people's shared world.

*Count: prose 523 + internal spaces 3 + paragraph allowance 49 + blocks 0 = 575.*

#### 7 of 21 · 559 / 637

Paul applies this language to Jesus' death and resurrection, events Christians understand to change humanity's relationship with God. The announcement therefore reaches beyond a hearer's private feelings. The word later also became the name for the first four books of the New Testament that tell Jesus' story: Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John. The message and the books are related uses of the same term.

Christ comes from the Greek word christos, meaning anointed one. It corresponds to the Hebrew title Messiah.

*Count: prose 506 + internal spaces 4 + paragraph allowance 49 + blocks 0 = 559.*

#### 8 of 21 · 623 / 637

Calling Jesus the Christ therefore places him within Israel's hopes for the one God would send; it is not a statement about his family name. That connection with Israel's story will become clearer as you read the rest of the library.

Resurrection means being raised from death to a new, bodily life. In this proclamation it does not mean returning as a ghost, resuming the same mortal life, or being reborn as another person. The distinction matters because Paul is announcing what Christians say happened to Jesus, rather than using death and life as interchangeable images.

### A message inside a letter

#### 10 of 21 · 576 / 637

These words occur inside a letter to an existing community. Paul is addressing people who already know the message and who have questions and disagreements. He recalls the death, burial, resurrection, and appearances because those claims belong to the proclamation they received together.

The passage is consequently brief. It is not a complete biography of Jesus, and Paul is not trying to tell every episode of his life here. He assumes a shared story and recalls its central events for a particular purpose within the letter.

### The community behind the letter

#### 12 of 21 · 533 / 637

That background helps us ask who held influence in the community and whose needs were overlooked. It does not tell us the private motive of every Corinthian.

### The Old Testament shelves

#### 4 of 15 · 560 / 637

The Law contains Genesis through Deuteronomy. These books tell Israel's founding story: creation, the ancestors, the Exodus from Egypt, and the covenant at Sinai. The stories provide the setting for the laws, and the laws describe the life of a covenant community in its own world. Reading them well means relating a command to that community rather than copying every rule directly into modern life. Begin with Genesis 1–3 and 12, followed by Exodus 1–3 and 20.

The History books run from Joshua through Esther.

### Every passage is a kind of writing

#### 1 of 17 · 630 / 637

Understanding a passage includes recognizing how it communicates. A historical account, a love poem, and a teaching story can all concern something true, but they make different kinds of claims. The Bible contains history, compressed summary, law, poetry, wisdom, prophecy, apocalyptic vision, parable, letter, and Gospel. ### History and summary

#### 3 of 17 · 615 / 637

Historical narrative names people and places and follows events in a sequence. It presents those events as having happened while selecting and emphasizing details to communicate their meaning. Samuel, Kings, and Acts contain this kind of writing. Selection itself is part of telling a history, rather than evidence that the writer has necessarily distorted it.

Some passages compress much longer stretches of time. Genealogies, brief accounts of a period, and phrases such as in those days provide an outline rather than a complete record of everything that happened.

*Count: prose 562 + internal spaces 4 + paragraph allowance 49 + blocks 0 = 615.*

#### 4 of 17 · 166 / 637

Genesis 5 and 11, Chronicles, and Judges 2 include such summaries. Treating every summary as a complete timeline asks it to supply information its form may not offer.

*Count: prose 165 + internal spaces 1 + paragraph allowance 0 + blocks 0 = 166.*

### Law, poetry, and wisdom

#### 5 of 17 · 589 / 637

Law communicates through commands and cases, including situations introduced with if a man. It describes how a covenant community should live in its setting. Understanding that setting helps avoid both copying every ancient rule directly and dismissing the entire collection without examining it.

Poetry communicates through images, paired lines, and feeling. When a psalm depicts hills clapping their hands, the image expresses creation's praise rather than describing a geological event. The claim belongs to the comparison the poem makes.

*Count: prose 536 + internal spaces 4 + paragraph allowance 49 + blocks 0 = 589.*

#### 6 of 17 · 417 / 637

Reading it as imagery takes its meaning seriously.

Wisdom sayings offer another kind of guidance. Proverbs often describes patterns in the way life works, giving readers reasons to act wisely. A general observation does not become a guarantee that the same outcome will occur in every case. That distinction matters when a reader tries to turn a proverb into a promise.

*Count: prose 365 + internal spaces 3 + paragraph allowance 49 + blocks 0 = 417.*

### Prophecy and vision

#### 7 of 17 · 588 / 637

Prophetic speech often introduces warnings and promises as the LORD's word to a particular audience. The prophets name present injustice, announce judgment, and speak of hope beyond it. Prediction belongs within that work, but it does not replace attention to the people and circumstances being addressed.

Apocalyptic writing develops its message through visions. Daniel 7–12 and Revelation use beasts, numbers, and cosmic imagery to portray power, evil, and God's ultimate victory. The symbolism makes a claim about reality through images.

### Parables, allegory, letters, and Gospels

#### 9 of 17 · 543 / 637

A parable presents a story in order to make a point. When Jesus begins with a man who had two sons, the point does not depend on identifying those sons as historical people. The story's meaning is what the listener is asked to consider.

Allegory assigns representational meanings to details. Paul explicitly uses the term in Galatians 4:24, but that does not authorize readers to turn every story into a set of hidden symbols. Look for evidence in the text before assigning that kind of meaning.

*Count: prose 490 + internal spaces 4 + paragraph allowance 49 + blocks 0 = 543.*

#### 10 of 17 · 406 / 637

Letters address real recipients and their questions or conflicts. Their teaching belongs to an argument made in that situation. The Gospels, in turn, present Jesus' life in the form of ancient biography, selecting and arranging material to communicate its significance. They are not modern day-by-day chronicles. These differences explain why audience, sequence, and purpose deserve attention in each form.

### Where Christians read differently

#### 12 of 17 · 577 / 637

Some passages do not receive the same classification from every serious Christian reader.
