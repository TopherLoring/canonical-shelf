## Recover the conversation

### 1 of 10 · 454 / 637

Reading a letter can resemble hearing one side of a conversation. We know what the writer says, but the situation must be reconstructed carefully. Paul tells the Corinthians that their gatherings do harm, identifying divisions and an unequal meal where some are hungry while others have more than enough. These are stated details, not guesses about the background. They provide the starting point for identifying audience and argument before application.

## Locate the claim in context

### 3 of 10 · 590 / 637

Paul’s immediate concern is the treatment of people within a Christian gathering. He challenges humiliation and disregard for those who have little. The following verses recount the Lord’s Supper, and the later instruction is to wait for one another. His argument therefore connects the meal’s meaning with the treatment of participants rather than keeping ritual and social conduct separate.

An extracted phrase about eating and drinking can conceal this communal problem. Reading the surrounding argument restores what the phrase addresses.

## Keep the setting visible

### 5 of 10 · 630 / 637

Historical context can deepen understanding, provided confidence matches the evidence. The paragraph establishes unequal treatment, but it does not reveal every participant’s motive or supply a complete seating plan. A plausible historical reconstruction is still different from a direct statement in the passage. Label that difference instead of filling each unanswered question with certainty.

Build the interpretation in stages: identify writer and recipients, summarize the stated problem, trace the response, and mark what remains unknown. Only then consider a present concern.

### 7 of 10 · 449 / 637
