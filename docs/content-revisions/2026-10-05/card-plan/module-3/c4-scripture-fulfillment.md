## Jesus and Scripture: fulfillment is more than prediction

### 1 of 7 · 462 / 637

Fulfillment language can describe a promise, pattern, or divine purpose coming to realization. Its literary relationship varies from passage to passage. New Testament writers quote and allude to Israel’s Scriptures when presenting Jesus. Some connections involve promises oriented toward the future; others use an earlier event, image, person, or pattern to interpret a later event. Understanding the difference helps us see what a particular claim accomplishes.

## Locate the claim in context

### 3 of 7 · 584 / 637

Matthew 2 shows why fulfillment cannot be reduced to one category. Hosea 11 recalls Israel’s past Exodus with the line “Out of Egypt I called my son.” Matthew applies that line within the story of Jesus. The connection treats Israel’s history as a pattern. It does not require us to read Hosea’s earlier recollection as though it only predicted the itinerary of Jesus’ childhood.

Matthew also brings Jeremiah’s imagery of Rachel weeping into a new narrative setting. Jeremiah’s passage has its own earlier work before Matthew reuses it.

## Keep the setting visible

### 5 of 7 · 391 / 637

Ask two questions whenever a later writer uses an earlier passage: what does the earlier passage do in its own setting, and what does the later writer do with it here? Later intertextual study names prediction, typology, quotation, and allusion more precisely. For now, the two-context habit prevents a cross-reference from becoming the end of interpretation when it should be the beginning.
