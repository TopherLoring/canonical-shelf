## Translation spectrum: form, function, and readability

### 1 of 7 · 442 / 637

Formal and functional equivalence describe priorities within translation. A relatively formal rendering preserves source-language structures or repeated forms where readable English allows it. A more functional rendering aims to communicate the meaning naturally in English. A paraphrase may restate larger units more freely. Each approach involves choices about form, sense, idiom, and readability; no approach removes the need for judgment.

## Locate the claim in context

### 3 of 7 · 613 / 637

These categories describe tendencies rather than sealed boxes. The same translation can preserve form closely in one verse and recast it in another, because languages do not correspond word for word. Even a very literal rendering must choose meanings, reorder material, or supply words to make grammatical English.

Ask what a particular rendering preserves, clarifies, obscures, or interprets. A literal treatment of an idiom may retain the original image but leave its sense unclear. A functional treatment may communicate that sense while concealing the metaphor.

## Keep the setting visible

### 5 of 7 · 392 / 637

For serious study, consult more than one translation and read each translation’s own preface and notes. They help explain its aims and particular decisions. The spectrum is useful for diagnosing what a rendering tries to do, not for ranking a Bible’s holiness or guaranteeing its accuracy in every verse. Evaluate the passage and the evidence before extending a judgment to an entire version.
