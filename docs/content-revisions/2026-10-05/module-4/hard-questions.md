## Stay honest with difficult questions {#orient}

Questions about evil and suffering are intellectual and personal at the same time. Luke 13 challenges the inference that victims suffered because they were worse sinners than others. The guide distinguishes wrongdoing enabled by human freedom from disease and disasters within a dynamic natural world, while acknowledging that this does not explain why God permits every loss. Our objective is to address suffering, difficult ethical texts, and religious difference through evidence and care. Reading directly and admitting what remains unresolved protects inquiry from adding unsupported accusations to someone's pain.

::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}

God can bring healing and good from suffering without making the suffering good in itself or every tragedy a deliberate lesson. Lament, uncertainty, practical help, and hope can therefore belong together. Biblical miracles deserve serious study alongside differing interpretations, but neither a diagnosis nor an unhealed condition establishes possession or inadequate faith. An account of God's action cannot simply be assumed from the fact of suffering.

Conquest, slavery, and restrictions on women likewise require direct examination. In Philemon, study Paul's appeal to receive Onesimus as a brother while distinguishing the actual wording from what it leaves unresolved about slavery. Historical context helps explain a situation; it does not automatically excuse harm or settle its moral evaluation. A responsible argument records the explicit statements, the interpretation, the moral question, and the proposed application separately so that their relationship can be assessed.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}

Canonical Shelf explicitly affirms LGBTQ dignity, relationships, marriage, and equal participation in ministry. It presents opposing interpretations and affirming responses with attention to context without putting people's dignity on trial. Present-day application is examined through Jesus' teaching, love of neighbor, and human dignity. These commitments require accountable reasoning, not an undefined appeal to a desirable conclusion.

Respect for other religions includes Christianity's Jewish roots and rejection of collective Jewish blame for Jesus' death. Romans 11 challenges the claim that God simply abandoned Israel. The guide teaches salvation through Christ while explaining disagreements about covenant and the unevangelized, without pronouncing particular people's final destiny. Acknowledging uncertainty preserves the limit of the evidence. In the disaster scenario that follows, listening and practical support can begin while an unsupported accusation is examined and corrected. Neither care nor honest inquiry needs to wait for a complete cosmic explanation.
