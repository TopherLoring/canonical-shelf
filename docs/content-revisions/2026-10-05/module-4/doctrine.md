## Speak carefully about God {#orient}

Christian doctrine gathers and interprets biblical testimony through reasoned reflection. Three recurring terms ask different questions: Trinity concerns who God is, incarnation concerns the Son becoming human, and providence concerns God's purposes in history. The historic Trinity confesses one God in three persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. It affirms unity and real distinction together, so neither three independent gods nor one person merely changing roles states the teaching accurately.

Canonical Shelf also identifies its own governing convictions. God is the loving Creator and source of all existence; God is Love and cannot act contrary to Love. Created beings have genuine agency, so God does not determine every choice or directly cause every action. This conviction about Love and freedom governs the guide's discussion of providence, forgiveness, judgment, and reconciliation. Historic Christian formulations are taught alongside that position for religious literacy, without claiming that they all give the same account.

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}

Incarnation names the Son's becoming human in Jesus Christ. Historic Christianity confesses Jesus as fully divine and fully human. A half-divine, half-human mixture would reduce both claims, so it does not explain the confession. John's account of the Word and Philippians' account of self-giving provide primary readings with which to examine this language. Begin with their testimony rather than treating the later doctrinal formulation as a quotation from either passage.

Teaching about the Spirit concerns life, prayer, community, and God's work among people. Romans 8 describes divine help amid human weakness and hope. That setting matters for discernment: understanding the Spirit does not require treating every intense feeling as a direct command from God. Responsibility remains necessary when evaluating the claim and its effects on other people.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}

Providence concerns God's purposes and care within history. The guide affirms meaningful human response: grace can be accepted or resisted, while human choices cannot ultimately thwart God's purpose. Foreknowledge means knowing beforehand; predestination concerns an appointed outcome; determinism concerns how events are determined. The concepts are related differently in different systems, but their meanings cannot simply be exchanged.

Analogies should be evaluated by the claims they explain. Water changing states can obscure the distinction of persons, while dividing an object into thirds can imply that each person is only part of God. State the doctrine, identify the feature an analogy illuminates, and acknowledge where it fails. Mystery makes room for the limits of explanation without excusing careless reasoning. The objective is to distinguish these questions and represent their answers accurately; participation does not require personal assent.
