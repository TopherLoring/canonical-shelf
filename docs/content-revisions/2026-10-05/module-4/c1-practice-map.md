## Christian practice: beliefs take a communal form {#orient}

Christian beliefs take shape in worship, prayer, baptism, Communion, care, and communal life. Christianity therefore involves more than agreeing with propositions. The New Testament depicts people learning, praying, sharing meals, caring for needs, baptizing, remembering Jesus, worshiping, and making ethical decisions together. These practices give belief a social and embodied form. Our objective is to recognize their different purposes and the connections between what a community teaches and how it lives.

::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}

Acts 2 offers an idealized summary of an early Jerusalem community. Teaching, fellowship, breaking bread, prayer, shared resources, and public life appear together. This arrangement shows that Christian formation—the shaping of habits, understanding, and character—involves relationships as well as individual learning. The summary describes a community at a particular moment; it is not a complete rulebook for every later congregation.

Baptism is the Christian water rite associated with initiation and life in Christ. Communion, also called Eucharist or the Lord's Supper, is the Christian shared meal. Traditions differ over the timing, meaning, admission, and relation to grace of these practices. Prayer likewise extends beyond requests. Praise, lament, confession, thanksgiving, intercession, and attentive listening all belong within Christian practice. Distinguishing their purposes helps us avoid treating every practice as the same kind of action.

## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}

This lesson introduces a landscape in which baptism, Communion, prayer, ethics, worship, ministry, and denominational differences can be examined more closely. A local practice cannot be assumed to represent every Christian community. Sacramental theology, governance, culture, history, and interpretation all help explain variation. Acts can reveal priorities and relationships without requiring mechanical reproduction of every economic arrangement. The question to retain is how a practice gives communal form to belief, and what further evidence is needed before making it a universal requirement.
