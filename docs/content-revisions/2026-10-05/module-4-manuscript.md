## Theology: the claims you will keep meeting

Lesson ID: c1-theology-map

**Objective:** Build an introductory map of the central Christian claims that later courses examine in depth.

**Primary reading:** Matthew 28:16–20

### Theology: the claims you will keep meeting

Christian theology is reasoned reflection about God and the claims of faith. It asks connected questions about God, Jesus Christ, the Holy Spirit, humanity, sin, salvation, the Church, resurrection, and final hope. Our aim is to recognize those questions before trying to settle their disputed answers. An adult beginning Scripture does not need a conclusion on every controversy, but does need words that identify what a controversy concerns. A question about who Jesus is differs from a question about how salvation is received, even when Christians connect their answers.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Matthew 28:16–20 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Matthew's closing commission names Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together. Historic Christianity draws this testimony together with other passages in confessing one God and speaking of Father, Son, and Spirit. It also confesses Jesus as fully divine and fully human. Trinity and incarnation are later doctrinal terms for these claims. A term can summarize an argument across Scripture without being a quotation from the passage we are reading. We therefore distinguish what a passage directly says from the explanation Christians develop by bringing passages together.

The same Christian story connects human dignity with sin, Christ's death and resurrection with grace, and repentance and faith with life in Christian community. It speaks of the Spirit's work, bodily resurrection, judgment, and renewed creation. Traditions may share these words while disagreeing about mechanisms, emphasis, and practical implications. Recognizing a shared word begins comparison; it does not prove that the answers are identical.

### Keep the setting visible

To follow these connections responsibly, distinguish the biblical text from historical evidence, interpretation, doctrine, reception, and application. Reception means how a passage or teaching has been understood by later communities; application asks how it bears on life. The introductory map locates the questions, study of biblical history and primary passages supplies context, and synthesis compares positions and difficult cases. These are related tasks rather than interchangeable kinds of evidence. Return to the objective when a debate becomes confusing: identify the question, find its evidence, and then examine the reasoning that connects the two.

### Glossary

**Theology.** Theology is reasoned reflection about God and the claims of faith. It connects questions about God, humanity, salvation, community, and final hope.

**Trinity.** The Trinity is the historic Christian confession of one God in three persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. It preserves unity and real distinction rather than dividing God into three beings or parts.

**Incarnation.** Incarnation names the Christian claim that the Son became genuinely human in Jesus Christ. Historic teaching affirms full humanity and full divinity, rather than a disguise or mixture of incomplete halves.

### Additional questions

#### Central versus disputed

Trinity, incarnation, Christ’s death and resurrection, and resurrection hope are historic Christian centers. Christians still disagree about predestination, sacramental mechanisms, atonement models, final punishment, end-times timelines, and many applications.

#### Understanding is not assent

Course challenges test whether you can represent a claim and its evidence. They do not grade whether you personally believe it.

### Deeper reading note

A doctrine can summarize a broad scriptural argument without being a quotation from one verse. That is why later study must ask both what individual passages say and how communities have drawn them together.

## Trinity: one God, real distinction

Lesson ID: trinity-language

**Objective:** Distinguish the historic Trinity from three gods, three parts, and one person changing roles.

**Primary reading:** Matthew 28:16–20

### Trinity: one God, real distinction

The historic doctrine of the Trinity confesses one God in three distinct persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Learning it means understanding how unity and distinction are held together, rather than imagining three gods, three pieces of God, or one person changing costumes. Most historic Christian churches use this classical formulation. The course teaches it so that you can recognize and discuss what those churches claim.

Canonical Shelf's own Statement of Faith takes a different position. It affirms one God with one divine consciousness and understands Father, Son, and Holy Spirit as Scripture's language for how that one God is present and relates to creation. It does not understand them as separate divine consciousnesses or literal family relationships. It affirms that the divine reality embodied in Jesus existed eternally within God and participated in creation, and that the Holy Spirit is God's own presence throughout creation. These claims belong to the guide's own position. Labeling them makes the difference from the classical account visible rather than implying that all Christians teach the same thing.

Matthew's closing commission names Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together in baptism. Begin with that testimony. Historic Trinitarian teaching brings it into conversation with other biblical passages; it does not claim that the complete later definition appears in this one sentence.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Matthew 28:16–20 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

In the historic confession, person names a real distinction within the one God. It does not simply mean three separate human individuals enlarged to cosmic size. That qualification matters because an explanation that divides God into three independent beings loses the unity the doctrine affirms. An explanation that removes every distinction loses what the language of Father, Son, and Spirit is intended to protect.

Calling each person one third of God therefore changes the claim: a third is only a component, whereas the confession does not divide divinity into components. The costume comparison changes it in another direction. One person wearing different costumes conveys a single actor but removes real distinction. These near misses show why an analogy must be judged by the doctrine it is meant to explain. Adding detail to a misleading image does not repair the mistaken claim at its center.

### Keep the setting visible

Understanding these boundaries does not mean making God fully comprehensible. Mystery acknowledges a limit to understanding; it does not prevent us from noticing when an explanation contradicts its own definition. A beginner can describe the historic formulation, identify why a particular analogy fails, and distinguish the guide's position without claiming to resolve every theological question. That is the purpose of the checks that follow. They assess representation and reasoning, not personal assent to either account.

## The Spirit: help, character, and discernment

Lesson ID: spirit-discernment

**Objective:** Connect teaching about the Spirit with observable character while avoiding certainty based only on intensity of feeling.

**Primary reading:** Galatians 5:16–26

### The Spirit: help, character, and discernment

Galatians contrasts destructive works with the fruit of the Spirit. Fruit is a metaphor for what a life produces, directing attention to conduct rather than only to dramatic experiences. Our question is how teaching about the Spirit can inform discernment: the careful testing of a claim by its content and effects. A strong feeling is an experience to consider, but its intensity alone does not establish that God is its source. The passage gives us a reason to examine what follows from a claim to spiritual guidance.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Galatians 5:16–26 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Paul names love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. English translations sometimes use different terms. Compare their wording while asking whether they are describing the same moral concern; a changed English word does not automatically mean a changed teaching. Self-control concerns responsibility for one's own conduct, not a warrant to dominate another person.

The final warning against conceit, provocation, and envy shows why the list belongs in a discussion of community. A person who invokes spiritual guidance while treating neighbors with contempt cannot make that ethical question disappear by describing a powerful experience. The claim must be examined alongside conduct, responsibility, and the wider scriptural conversation. Even the fruit list can be misused if someone turns it into grounds for boasting about spiritual superiority.

### Keep the setting visible

This test of conduct is not a salvation score. Nor does every difficult emotion prove spiritual failure. The passage directs attention toward growth, which can include acknowledging harm, seeking help, and practicing a different response. Romans 8 also places the Spirit's work alongside human weakness and dependence, so difficulty should not simply be equated with divine absence. Our objective is to connect spiritual claims with observable character. In a difficult conversation, ask what the claim encourages you to do and how that action treats the other person; understanding this connection does not require pretending never to feel frustration.

## Atonement: several images of one saving work

Lesson ID: atonement-images

**Objective:** Distinguish reconciliation, substitution, and victory without reducing the cross to a moral example.

**Primary reading:** 2 Corinthians 5:18–21

### Atonement: several images of one saving work

Atonement names Christ's saving work in addressing sin and reconciliation with God. Reconciliation means restoring a relationship across estrangement. In 2 Corinthians, Paul attributes that restoration to God's initiative through Christ. The direction of the action matters: humanity is not presented as first making itself worthy and then persuading God to act. Our objective is to distinguish several images of this saving work without reducing the cross to an example of kindness that people could reproduce unaided.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** 2 Corinthians 5:18–21 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Paul connects Christ with sin and righteousness and those reconciled through him. Christians draw on this passage and others to explain representation, substitution, sacrifice, and exchange. Each explanation gathers evidence and reasons about it. A theological model therefore is not identical to a single metaphor or to every statement in the passage.

Penal substitution describes Christ as bearing the penalty due to sinners. Victory accounts emphasize the defeat of sin and death, while reconciliation emphasizes a restored relationship. These images address different aspects of the saving work and can overlap without being interchangeable. The guide accommodates penal substitution but does not make it the sole scriptural explanation of how salvation is accomplished. Comparing an image's emphasis with another image's emphasis is more informative than assuming that different words must describe mutually exclusive events.

### Keep the setting visible

Christ's necessary sacrifice and resurrection remain central to this account. The present lesson asks what Christ accomplishes; receiving grace asks how people receive the gift. Distinguishing those questions keeps an explanatory model from turning into a transaction diagram that exhausts salvation, and keeps moral example from replacing saving significance. Compare the reconciliation language here with the victory language in Colossians 2:13–15. Explain what each illuminates and where a larger theological argument is still needed. The comparison returns us to the same objective: several images can describe one saving work without any one image saying everything.

## Receiving grace: repentance and trust

Lesson ID: receiving-grace

**Objective:** Distinguish receiving salvation from earning it and human dependence from lack of human value.

**Primary reading:** Ephesians 2:1–10

### Receiving grace: repentance and trust

Grace means God's freely given favor and saving gift. Ephesians moves from bondage and death toward God's mercy and life with Christ, giving divine initiative priority. Salvation is therefore not described as a prize for people who have already repaired themselves. We will distinguish the gift, its reception, and the changed conduct that follows, while explaining why dependence on help does not make a person worthless. This distinction matters wherever faith is confused with a new way to earn a reward.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Ephesians 2:1–10 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Grace describes the gift-character of salvation; faith describes trust in and reception of God's saving work through Christ. Faith is not a competing currency used to purchase what works could not buy. In this guide, acknowledging sin, repenting, and accepting Christ's saving gift belong to receiving grace. Repentance is a turning from wrongdoing, not a claim that a person has now made God owe them salvation.

Ephesians denies grounds for boasting. If salvation were owed because of achievement, it could become a measure of superiority over other people. The text instead appeals to God's love and mercy. Those appeals belong within the explanation: inability to merit salvation must not be changed into a claim that human beings lack value. Needing God's saving initiative and being loved by God are not contradictory descriptions.

### Keep the setting visible

The passage then speaks of good works as the purpose of the changed life. Its order explains the connection: being created for a changed life differs from being saved by accumulating achievements. Conduct matters as a response to grace without becoming its purchase price. When explaining the gospel, keep gift, reception, and ethical result distinguishable and related. Denying a merit contest does not erase responsibility, and affirming responsibility does not make salvation a tally of deeds. The question to carry into practice is what receiving this gift calls a person to do, rather than how much that action makes the gift worth.

## After forgiveness: love takes practical form

Lesson ID: grace-repair

**Objective:** Distinguish a response to grace from payment for welcome and salvation.

**Primary reading:** Luke 19:1–10

### After forgiveness: love takes practical form

Grace and practical repair belong together, but making amends does not buy welcome or salvation. Luke's encounter with Zacchaeus unfolds through seeking, meeting Jesus, welcome, complaint, and concern about possessions and wrongdoing. Read this sequence before treating a statement about money as an abstract formula. Jesus' approach and the crowd's objection belong to the story's meaning. Our task is to distinguish a response to grace from a payment that supposedly makes God willing to save.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Luke 19:1–10 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

Zacchaeus speaks about giving and repayment. Readers debate the statement's tense and implications, but its ethical concern is evident: money and the effects of wrongdoing matter in this encounter. A religious experience cannot be detached from the neighbors affected by one's conduct. Restitution means returning what was taken or compensating for a concrete loss where possible.

The guide treats repair as a response to grace. Acknowledging the people harmed makes repentance more credible, because wrongdoing has consequences beyond the offender's feelings. Forgiveness, however, does not entitle an offender to demand immediate trust or unrestricted access to the person injured. Trust concerns whether someone can safely be relied upon; it need not be restored merely because forgiveness is being considered.

Canonical Shelf's own account of repentance has four parts: recognizing that an action contradicted God's loving purpose, taking responsibility without deflection or self-condemnation, apologizing, and genuinely intending a different outcome. Whether the later change succeeds is not what makes the repentance genuine. Asking for forgiveness can express repentance and relationship, but does not make God willing to forgive. Shame can prevent a person from asking; Love does not withhold forgiveness because a wounded person cannot find the right words. This is the guide's stated account, distinguished from what the narrative alone establishes.

### Keep the setting visible

Practical repair can include admission, restitution where possible, changed habits, and respect for boundaries that protect others. Different harms call for different responses, so an apology is not a universal remedy for every loss. Forgiveness, repaired material harm, and rebuilt trust are connected but distinct. The lesson establishes a theological relationship between grace and conduct; it does not promise that every damaged relationship can be restored on demand. Carry this distinction into the reflection: an apology should attend to the affected person's needs, rather than making their immediate trust a condition of the offender's willingness to change.

## Cross, resurrection, and the gift of grace

Lesson ID: cross-grace

**Objective:** Distinguish Christ’s saving work, receiving grace, and the response of discipleship.

**Primary reading:** 1 Corinthians 15:1–8

### Cross, resurrection, and the gift of grace

Christian proclamation holds Jesus' death and resurrection together. Paul recalls that Christ died for sins, was buried, was raised, and appeared to witnesses. This is a proclamation about saving work, not only admiration for a kind person. Humanity is not presented as accomplishing that saving work for itself. We will distinguish what Christ accomplishes, how grace is received, and how discipleship responds, so that neither the gift nor the responsibility it invites disappears from the account.

### Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** 1 Corinthians 15:1–8 (BSB).

### Locate the claim in context

The guide teaches that sin separates humanity from God and brings humanity under judgment. Through his willing sacrifice and resurrection, Jesus bears our sins, confronts their consequences, defeats sin and death, and makes reconciliation with God possible. Reconciliation means restoring a ruptured relationship. These are connected theological claims about the significance of Christ's work, and the New Testament expresses them through several themes rather than one exhaustive picture.

Penal substitution describes Christ as bearing the penalty due to sinners. Other accounts emphasize victory over sin and death, sacrifice, representation, healing, and reconciliation. The themes can overlap because they illuminate different dimensions of salvation. Canonical Shelf accommodates penal substitution without making its particular account of punishment the sole explanation. Paul's sequence in the reading identifies the proclaimed events; it does not by itself select one exclusive mechanism of atonement.

### Keep the setting visible

Salvation is God's gift of grace, made possible through Christ's sacrifice and resurrection. In this guide, people receive it by acknowledging sin and their inability to save themselves, repenting, and trusting Christ's saving work. ## Millennium and rapture: name the frameworks before debating them

### Millennium and rapture: name the frameworks before debating them

Revelation 20 describes a thousand-year reign, and Christian interpreters organize that imagery in several ways. Premillennial approaches place Christ's return before a future millennium. Postmillennial approaches expect a period of gospel-shaped flourishing before Christ's return. Amillennial approaches read the millennium symbolically or as the present reign of Christ and the saints rather than a future earthly thousand-year phase. Our objective is to name these frameworks before debating them, while keeping their disputed sequences distinct from the broader Christian emphasis on resurrection and final hope.

### Read the passage with the question in view

A label that identifies Christ's return as preceding the millennium therefore does not yet establish all of these additional relationships.

Popular rapture teaching often draws especially on 1 Thessalonians 4 together with a dispensational scheme. Christians disagree about whether the catching-up passage describes a separate secret removal, the public coming of Christ, or another configuration.
