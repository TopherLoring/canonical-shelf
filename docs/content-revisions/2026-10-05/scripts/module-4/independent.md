# Complete an independent reading project

Lesson ID: independent

**Objective:** Build and explain a contextual reading that distinguishes evidence, interpretation, disagreement, and application.

**Primary reading:** Jude 1:20–25

## Complete an independent reading project

An introductory guide should eventually help you read with less dependence on the guide. Begin an independent project with a manageable whole book such as Ruth, Jonah, Philippians, Philemon, or Jude. Read the complete book in Explore before concentrating on a few verses. Record an initial question without deciding its answer in advance. Our objective is to build a contextual reading whose evidence, interpretation, disagreement, and application can be explained and reviewed, including what remains uncertain.

## Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Jude 1:20–25 (BSB).

## Locate the claim in context

Build an evidence record by identifying the kind of writing, the audience where known, repeated concerns, important turns, and details that complicate your first impression. Separate the text's explicit statements from historical reconstructions and personal associations. A reconstruction infers missing circumstances from evidence; it should not be presented as if the text directly supplied them. Investigate a factual claim with a reliable source when it matters to the argument.

Next explain the passage's meaning in context and compare at least one alternative interpretation. State why another reader could find that account persuasive rather than merely listing it as an error. The type of disagreement helps determine the work needed: a translation difference may call for attention to wording or manuscript notes, while a theological disagreement may require comparing several passages. Explain both what the evidence establishes and what it does not settle.

## Keep the setting visible

Finally propose a concrete application and consider its effects on other people. The goal is not to make Scripture confirm every first preference, but to develop an accountable reading that can be revised. Return to the text, the people affected, and the wider Christian conversation when evidence or concerns change. An application remains open to examination even when its intentions are good.

The project is complete when you can explain the reasoning, not when every question has disappeared. Use the optional reflection to draft a five-part brief: question, context, evidence, interpretation with an alternative, and application with a limit. The checks assess this method rather than grading personal conclusions or certifying theology. In Jude, read the whole letter after its selected ending and compare mercy with its warnings; the final blessing alone cannot replace the connected argument. Continued reading, community, and reflection remain part of learning.

**Check: Construct your study project**

Build a responsible sequence from first encounter to application.

1. Propose an application and name its limits
2. Read the complete book and identify context
3. Form an interpretation and compare an alternative
4. Record evidence, including details that complicate your first impression

**Answer order:** Read the complete book and identify context → Record evidence, including details that complicate your first impression → Form an interpretation and compare an alternative → Propose an application and name its limits

**Hint:** First encounter the work, then build the evidence, reason from it, and apply it.

**Explanation:** A reading project makes its reasoning visible: context, evidence, interpretation with alternatives, and accountable application.

**Check: Defend and revise your reading**

You have drafted a reading project. A peer raises evidence that challenges your conclusion.

**Situation 1:** How do you handle the new evidence?

1. Hide it so the project looks confident
2. Return to the passage and assess its relevance
3. Assume disagreement proves the peer is insincere

**Answer:** Return to the passage and assess its relevance

**Feedback by option:**

1. Completeness includes acknowledging evidence that complicates the argument.
2. Your reading remains answerable to the text.
3. Motive judgments do not resolve the interpretive issue.

**Situation 2:** The evidence supports more than one interpretation. What belongs in the project?

1. Your preferred conclusion presented as the only possible view
2. A claim that no interpretation can ever be evaluated
3. Your reasoning, a fair alternative, and the remaining uncertainty

**Answer:** Your reasoning, a fair alternative, and the remaining uncertainty

**Feedback by option:**

1. That would hide a real limitation.
2. Uncertainty does not make all arguments equally strong.
3. This makes the interpretation reviewable and proportionate to the evidence.

**Situation 3:** Someone affected by your proposed application says it is causing harm. What next?

1. Listen, examine the effects, and revise where warranted
2. Treat the mission score as proof the application is right
3. Ignore their experience because your intentions were good

**Answer:** Listen, examine the effects, and revise where warranted

**Feedback by option:**

1. Responsible application includes accountability to the people affected.
2. A learning score cannot validate every real-world judgment.
3. Intentions matter but do not erase consequences.

**Hint:** Keep textual evidence, competing explanations, and lived consequences open to examination.

**Explanation:** A completed project demonstrates accountable reasoning, not immunity from revision.

## Reflect on the lesson
**Optional reflection**

Draft your project: question; context; two pieces of evidence; interpretation and an alternative; application and one limit.

**Model response:** For Jude, I might ask how warning and mercy fit together, read the full letter, distinguish its audiences and appeals, compare readings, and propose a response that does not turn disagreement into contempt.

## Glossary

**Evidence record.** An evidence record gathers textual observations and sourced contextual information. It keeps explicit statements identifiable alongside reconstructions and unanswered questions.

**Alternative interpretation.** An alternative interpretation is another reasoned account of a passage’s meaning. A fair comparison explains why a reader could find it persuasive.

**Synthesis.** Synthesis is a connected explanation that brings observations and distinctions into a coherent account. Its reasoning should remain open to review and revision.

## Deeper reading note

Read all of Jude after the selected ending. Compare the call to mercy with the letter’s warnings. How does the whole letter complicate a summary based only on its final blessing?
