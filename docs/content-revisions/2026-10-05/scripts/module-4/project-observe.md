## Project lab 1: observe a whole book

Begin with a manageable book and read it completely in Explore. Philemon's short length permits repeated reading, making it useful for a first project. Its selected opening introduces names, relationships, an address, and thanksgiving; the full letter supplies the appeal. We will build a book profile that distinguishes what the text presents from background we have assumed. Reading the opening carefully is a beginning, but a whole-book summary requires the whole book.

## Locate the claim in context

Give the profile five fields: summary, people, opening move, authorial claim, and audience. Record the book's own statements separately from traditional attribution or scholarly reconstruction. A familiar title does not always provide a complete account of authorship or audience, and a salutation may name participants without explaining every circumstance or relationship. The rest of the letter can therefore correct a first profile.

Locate the book on the shelf and identify its group. Then distinguish its narrative setting from its composition date. These answer different questions: the circumstances described and the time of writing are not automatically identical. The integrated Content, Groups, Order, and Chronology missions reinforce these distinctions across the 66 books. A stronger profile keeps each kind of information identifiable instead of letting one stand in for the others.

## Keep the setting visible

Include references in your evidence notebook and record at least one observation that surprised you. A profile should expose uncertainty rather than conceal it through guesses. If a field cannot yet be completed, mark the uncertainty and identify a passage or source that could help investigate it. This is part of the objective, not a failure to make the profile look finished. The final question is whether each statement is an observation with evidence, a labeled inference, or something still needing investigation.

## Glossary

**Book profile.** A book profile records a book’s content, people, opening, authorial information, and audience. References and labeled uncertainties help separate observation from assumed background.

**Observation.** An observation is a statement about what the text actually presents. It should be distinguished from an inference about missing circumstances.

**Reconstruction.** A reconstruction is an account of missing context inferred from evidence. It should be labeled as an inference rather than presented as the text’s direct statement.
