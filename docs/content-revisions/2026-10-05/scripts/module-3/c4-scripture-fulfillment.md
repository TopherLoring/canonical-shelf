# Jesus and Scripture: fulfillment is more than prediction

Lesson ID: c4-scripture-fulfillment

**Objective:** Distinguish direct prediction from typology, quotation, allusion, and narrative rereading when New Testament writers connect Jesus with Israel’s Scriptures.

**Primary reading:** Matthew 2:13–18

## Jesus and Scripture: fulfillment is more than prediction

Fulfillment language can describe a promise, pattern, or divine purpose coming to realization. Its literary relationship varies from passage to passage. New Testament writers quote and allude to Israel’s Scriptures when presenting Jesus. Some connections involve promises oriented toward the future; others use an earlier event, image, person, or pattern to interpret a later event. Understanding the difference helps us see what a particular claim accomplishes.

## Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Matthew 2:13–18 (BSB).

## Locate the claim in context

Matthew 2 shows why fulfillment cannot be reduced to one category. Hosea 11 recalls Israel’s past Exodus with the line “Out of Egypt I called my son.” Matthew applies that line within the story of Jesus. The connection treats Israel’s history as a pattern. It does not require us to read Hosea’s earlier recollection as though it only predicted the itinerary of Jesus’ childhood.

Matthew also brings Jeremiah’s imagery of Rachel weeping into a new narrative setting. Jeremiah’s passage has its own earlier work before Matthew reuses it. A Christian theological connection can be meaningful without claiming that the earlier author consciously described every later event in advance. Preserving both contexts makes the later interpretation clearer rather than depriving it of significance.

## Keep the setting visible

Ask two questions whenever a later writer uses an earlier passage: what does the earlier passage do in its own setting, and what does the later writer do with it here? Later intertextual study names prediction, typology, quotation, and allusion more precisely. For now, the two-context habit prevents a cross-reference from becoming the end of interpretation when it should be the beginning.

## Glossary

**Fulfillment.** Fulfillment describes Scripture, promise, pattern, or divine purpose coming to realization. The literary relationship must be examined in each passage.

**Typology.** Typology reads a later person or event as corresponding to an earlier scriptural pattern. It does not require a simple direct prediction.

**Allusion.** An allusion is an indirect echo or reference to another text. Its identification depends on evidence in wording and context.

## Additional questions

### A two-context habit

Always open the earlier passage and the later passage. A cross-reference label is the start of interpretation, not the end.

## Deeper reading note

Christian traditions and scholars debate categories such as sensus plenior, typology, figural reading, prophecy, and authorial intention. The essential discipline is to preserve both textual contexts before making a theological synthesis.
