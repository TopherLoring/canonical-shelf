# Prediction, typology, quotation, and allusion

Lesson ID: c5-typology-allusion

**Objective:** Classify major ways later biblical texts reuse earlier Scripture and explain why those relationships require two-context reading.

**Primary reading:** Matthew 2:13–18

## Prediction, typology, quotation, and allusion

Intertextual interpretation studies relationships between texts. Biblical writers make these relationships in several ways: direct quotation, indirect echo, a recurring pattern, a claim of fulfillment, or an argument that gathers several passages. Calling all of them prophecy hides distinctions we need in order to explain what the later writer does. The aim is to classify the relationship while preserving both texts’ contexts.

## Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Matthew 2:13–18 (BSB).

## Locate the claim in context

Direct prediction concerns an earlier passage oriented toward a future event or figure. Typology identifies a correspondence between earlier and later persons or events within a larger story. An allusion is a less explicit echo, recognized through wording, imagery, and context. A quotation reproduces or explicitly cites earlier wording. These descriptions identify different features of textual reuse rather than making every reuse a prediction.

Matthew’s use of Hosea’s memory of the Exodus for Jesus shows why the distinctions matter. Hosea 11 recalls Israel’s earlier story. Matthew brings that story into his account of Jesus. The connection can carry theological meaning without requiring Hosea’s passage to have functioned only as a prediction of Jesus’ childhood.

## Keep the setting visible

Read twice in a deliberate sense: first explain the earlier passage’s literary and historical work, then examine the later author’s reuse of it. Only after both are visible should a broader theological synthesis follow. Likewise, a possible allusion needs evidence beyond a resemblance. This two-context practice keeps a later interpretation from silently canceling the earlier meaning.

## Glossary

**Quotation.** A quotation explicitly cites or reproduces earlier wording. The later context still needs interpretation.

**Allusion.** An allusion is an indirect textual echo or reference. Resemblance alone is not enough to establish it confidently.

**Typology.** Typology identifies a later correspondence with an earlier scriptural person, event, institution, or pattern. It preserves the earlier context while examining the later connection.

## Additional questions

### A practical test for allusion

Look for distinctive wording, repeated clusters, thematic fit, availability to the later author, and whether the proposed source actually improves the reading. One shared common word is weak evidence.

## Deeper reading note

Scholars debate thresholds for identifying allusions and the relationship between authorial intent, reader recognition, canonical interpretation, and theological claims. Strong allusion arguments normally use multiple converging signals rather than resemblance alone.
