# Recover the conversation

Lesson ID: audience

**Objective:** Identify audience, argument, and missing information before applying a passage.

**Primary reading:** 1 Corinthians 11:17–22

## Recover the conversation

Reading a letter can resemble hearing one side of a conversation. We know what the writer says, but the situation must be reconstructed carefully. Paul tells the Corinthians that their gatherings do harm, identifying divisions and an unequal meal where some are hungry while others have more than enough. These are stated details, not guesses about the background. They provide the starting point for identifying audience and argument before application.

## Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** 1 Corinthians 11:17–22 (BSB).

## Locate the claim in context

Paul’s immediate concern is the treatment of people within a Christian gathering. He challenges humiliation and disregard for those who have little. The following verses recount the Lord’s Supper, and the later instruction is to wait for one another. His argument therefore connects the meal’s meaning with the treatment of participants rather than keeping ritual and social conduct separate.

An extracted phrase about eating and drinking can conceal this communal problem. Reading the surrounding argument restores what the phrase addresses. Canonical Shelf affirms an open table, but that commitment does not replace close reading or make every Christian tradition reach the same conclusion about Communion. Understanding Paul’s audience and concern gives us evidence with which to examine those interpretations.

## Keep the setting visible

Historical context can deepen understanding, provided confidence matches the evidence. The paragraph establishes unequal treatment, but it does not reveal every participant’s motive or supply a complete seating plan. A plausible historical reconstruction is still different from a direct statement in the passage. Label that difference instead of filling each unanswered question with certainty.

Build the interpretation in stages: identify writer and recipients, summarize the stated problem, trace the response, and mark what remains unknown. Only then consider a present concern. Cost, accessibility, timing, or social status may make a modern gathering’s welcome difficult to experience. Examining these conditions applies Paul’s concern with humiliation; it does not claim that he explicitly discusses each modern arrangement.

## Read closely

Return to Unit 1’s patronage lens and ask how unequal resources affect belonging at a shared meal. Treating each person’s eating as only a private choice can miss Paul’s explicit attention to hunger and humiliation. Social history helps us formulate questions about these relationships. It cannot supply every motive the letter leaves unstated. Keep the distinction between evidence and reconstruction in view as you explain the gathering’s problem.

## Glossary

**Audience.** The audience consists of the people a writing addresses. Identifying them helps recover the conversation.

**Literary context.** Literary context is the surrounding sentences, argument, and position within a work. It helps identify what an isolated phrase addresses.

**Historical reconstruction.** A historical reconstruction is an evidence-based proposal about the circumstances behind a text. It is not identical to a direct textual statement.
