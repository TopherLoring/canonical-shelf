# Before English: the Bible’s languages

Lesson ID: c1-bible-languages

**Objective:** Recognize Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek as the principal biblical languages and explain why translation is a normal part of reading Scripture.

**Primary reading:** Luke 4:16–21

## Before English: the Bible’s languages

English Bibles are translations of texts written in ancient languages. Most of the Old Testament is Hebrew, with smaller sections in Aramaic; the New Testament writings are Greek. Jesus and other first-century Jews inhabit a multilingual world in which Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek, and sometimes Latin serve different purposes and settings. Recognizing this linguistic world makes comparison of English wording a normal part of reading Scripture carefully.

## Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Luke 4:16–21 (BSB).

## Locate the claim in context

Luke describes Jesus reading Isaiah in a synagogue, but the Gospel reports that scene in Greek while referring to Israel’s Scriptures. The narrative already brings reception, quotation, and explanation of Scripture across languages into view. Translation is consequently neither an embarrassment to hide nor an unusual exception to scriptural reading.

Translators must judge how vocabulary, grammar, idiom, and style communicate to their audience. An English sentence cannot automatically display every feature of an ancient sentence. Acknowledging these choices does not make meaning unreachable; it explains why wording and notes deserve attention. Different translations can bring different features into view while still aiming to communicate the same passage responsibly.

## Keep the setting visible

If an interpretation depends heavily on one English word, compare translations and consult notes before drawing a large conclusion from it. Ask whether the difference concerns translation, underlying textual evidence, or interpretation. Careful study begins with such questions, even without fluency in Greek or Hebrew. Later translation study examines semantic range and equivalence more closely; for now, keep the mediation between an ancient text and its English reader visible.

## Glossary

**Hebrew.** Hebrew is the principal language of most of the Old Testament. English readers encounter these writings through translation.

**Aramaic.** Aramaic is a related Semitic language used in parts of the Old Testament and widely spoken in parts of the ancient Near East.

**Greek.** Greek is the language of the New Testament writings as they survive in the manuscript tradition.

## Additional questions

### Where is Aramaic in the Bible?

Substantial Aramaic passages occur in Ezra and Daniel, with smaller examples elsewhere. Course 5 can examine these boundaries and their historical setting.

### Do I need Greek or Hebrew?

No. Original-language study can sharpen questions, but careful use of multiple translations, notes, lexicons, and scholarly resources already improves interpretation. Knowing a little Greek or Hebrew is not a license to ignore context.

## Deeper reading note

Language history is more complicated than assigning one language to one people. Multilingualism, dialect, scribal practice, and translation traditions all matter. The introductory point is narrower: the biblical texts require linguistic mediation, and responsible study keeps that visible.
