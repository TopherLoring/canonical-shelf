# Project lab 2: trace an argument and a theme

Lesson ID: project-interpret

**Objective:** Construct an interpretation from evidence and compare a plausible alternative.

**Primary reading:** Philemon 1:8–16

## Project lab 2: trace an argument and a theme

This second project lab develops an interpretation from the whole-letter profile already completed. Read Paul’s appeal in Philemon and identify the response he seeks. Then ask how his language of relationship supports that request. An argument map makes those connections visible, whereas a collection of appealing phrases can merely confirm the conclusion a reader already prefers.

## Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Philemon 1:8–16 (BSB).

## Locate the claim in context

Connect observations to a claim and explain the warrant for each link. A warrant is the reason that particular evidence supports that conclusion. The same statement may support a limited interpretation without establishing every broader ethical claim. Include evidence that complicates the first reading, so the argument’s limits remain available for examination.

Next, trace one of the course’s fourteen themes into another book. Mercy in Philemon can be compared with mercy in a parable, for example, but its role must be examined in each setting. A thread connects developing concerns without making all passages say the same thing. Repeated wording by itself does not establish that the arguments are identical.

## Keep the setting visible

Write a short alternative interpretation that an informed reader could recognize as fair. Explain why the evidence favors your reading, what remains uncertain, and what could change your mind. The integrated Themes and Content missions provide broader practice; this project asks you to state the reasons yourself. The resulting interpretation should show both its support and its conditions for revision rather than concealing a difficult detail.

## Glossary

**Warrant.** A warrant explains why evidence supports a particular claim. An argument map should make that reasoning visible.

**Counterevidence.** Counterevidence challenges or complicates an interpretation. Including it helps identify a claim’s limits.

**Theme.** A theme is a concern traced across passages. Its development must be examined without erasing differences between settings.
