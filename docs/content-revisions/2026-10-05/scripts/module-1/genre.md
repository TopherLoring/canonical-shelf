# Practice: reading Psalm 23 as poetry

Lesson ID: genre

**Objective:** Use literary clues to interpret imagery without dismissing the passage’s claim.

**Primary reading:** Psalm 23:1–6

## Put the kinds of writing to work
Psalm 23 portrays the LORD as a shepherd and places the speaker in a dark valley. Both belong to its account of trust. Reading the poem well means explaining how its images communicate God's care while danger remains present.

The earlier lesson introduced kinds of writing; here we will practice with one poem and compare it with narrative, commands, and a vision. A recipe, a news report, and a love poem can communicate truth through different conventions, just as biblical stories, laws, poems, proverbs, letters, and visions do. A book can also mix forms: a narrative may quote a poem, and a letter may include a confession or hymn. The passage's own clues will guide the reading.

## Read the psalm slowly
> **Read:** Psalm 23:1–6 (BSB).

Read the psalm twice. First notice its pictures: the shepherd, green pastures, still water, the dark valley, the table, oil, and the cup. Then notice the speaker's relationship to God. Partway through, the speaker moves from talking about the LORD to addressing him directly. The images and that change of address both help communicate the poem's trust.

## The shepherd and the host
The LORD is pictured first as a shepherd and then as a host. Through those images the speaker describes being led, restored, accompanied through danger, and welcomed to a table. The comparison explains care and trust by giving readers something they can picture. Calling it a metaphor recognizes how the poem communicates its claim about God.

The valley and the enemies remain present. The speaker voices trust while danger still exists, rather than waiting until every threat has disappeared. That detail prevents the image of care from becoming a guarantee that faithful people will never experience hardship. An interpretation has to account for the danger as well as the provision.

The poem therefore gives language for confidence and dependence. It does not give readers a formula for predicting every outcome. Reading the whole passage keeps a statement about God's care connected with the situation in which that care is being confessed.

## Compare three other kinds of writing
The same attention to form changes how we read other passages. A proverb offers practical wisdom without guaranteeing the same result in every circumstance. A narrative may report a harmful action without endorsing it. An apocalyptic vision uses symbols to address crisis and hope. Recognizing the form begins the investigation; it does not settle every question of history or theology.

Use a routine that gives reasons for your interpretation. Identify the form, point to a clue, explain what its language communicates, and test that explanation against the whole passage. Vivid wording is not automatically a literal description, but difficult wording is not automatically a metaphor either. The reading needs evidence.

Acts 18:1–4 narrates Paul's arrival in Corinth, his work, and his conversations. Exodus 20:12–17 gives commands within the covenant. A command says what people should do, rather than reporting what everyone did. Daniel 7:2–3 describes beasts within a vision, and 7:17–18 interprets them in relation to kings and a kingdom. The passage itself therefore supplies a reason to understand the beasts symbolically.

These forms address reality in different ways. Daniel's vision concerns power and hope rather than zoology; Acts narrates a setting and actions; Exodus prescribes conduct. Symbolic language can have meaning, and narrative form can still leave historical questions to investigate. Returning to each passage's clues keeps those distinctions grounded in the writing.

## What you now know
You have read Psalm 23 by following the way a poem communicates. Its images portray care, the speaker begins addressing God directly, and the valley remains part of the scene. Those details support trust amid danger rather than a promise that trust removes every threat.

The comparison passages showed how narrative, commands, and visions make other kinds of claims. Readers broadly recognize Psalm 23 as a poem of trust while differing on some details, including whether the house of the LORD refers to the temple, God's presence, or both. The habit to carry forward is to identify the kind of writing and point to the clue that supports your judgment before deciding what the passage means.

## Glossary

**Genre.** Genre identifies the kind of writing and the expectations it creates. It helps a reader explain how a passage communicates its claim rather than assuming every form works like a report.

**Metaphor.** A metaphor describes one thing in terms of another. Calling the LORD a shepherd uses the responsibilities of a shepherd to communicate care and guidance; the image is part of the poem's meaning.

**Apocalyptic.** Apocalyptic writing uses revelatory visions and symbols to address judgment, crisis, and hope. Its images call for attention to the passage's own explanations and setting rather than a literal description of the creatures pictured.

## Deeper reading note

Compare the shepherd and host images. Which details change, and what claim about God remains? A useful analogy opens understanding while retaining limits; it should not turn every incidental feature of shepherding into a doctrine about God. Books can mix genres: Daniel includes court narratives and visions. Classify the passage before classifying the whole book. Revelation likewise uses symbolic visions, but its images must be read in their own literary and historical contexts; a genre label alone cannot identify a modern political figure or date.
