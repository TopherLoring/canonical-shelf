# Find your way: references, canon, and translation

Lesson ID: library

**Objective:** Read a Bible reference and distinguish canon, translation, and genre.

**Primary reading:** Luke 1:1–4

## Find your way
A Bible reference tells you where to find a passage. Canon tells you which writings belong to the collection you are using, and translation tells you how their language has been rendered. Distinguishing these questions helps explain why two Bibles may look different without either being fabricated.

The collection contains writings composed and preserved across many centuries. The Old Testament contains Israel's Scriptures; the New Testament includes four Gospels, Acts, letters, and Revelation. Christianity emerged within Judaism, so Israel's Scriptures should not be read as a failed preliminary version of Christianity. With that relationship in view, we can turn the earlier lessons on shelves and literary forms into a practical habit: locate a passage, identify the edition, and read the surrounding context.

## Read the passage with the question in view
> **Read:** Luke 1:1–4 (BSB).

Luke 1:1–4 introduces the writer's work before the account begins. Notice how he describes earlier accounts, testimony from witnesses, and his own investigation. That introduction gives you reasons to ask about sources, purpose, and audience rather than treating the book as a passage without a setting.

## Read a reference like an address
Luke 1:1–4 identifies the book of Luke, chapter 1, and verses 1 through 4. Find those parts in that order. Chapter and verse numbers make a passage easier to locate, but their divisions do not take the place of the story or argument the writer composed. Opening the surrounding paragraphs helps you read a verse within that flow.

The same address can appear in several styles. Luke 1:1–4 uses the full book name; Lk 1:1–4 abbreviates it. Some British and academic writing uses Luke 1.1–4, placing a period between chapter and verse. Each version points to the same four verses. Learning the notation keeps a difference in style from becoming a difference in what you think is being cited.

When an address names only one verse, read a few verses before and after it. An isolated sentence can appear to make a claim that its context qualifies or explains differently. Navigation should therefore end with attention to the passage, rather than stopping when you reach the right number.

## What is in the collection, and in what language
Canon names the collection a religious community recognizes as Scripture. This reader uses the 66-book Protestant canon and the Berean Standard Bible translation. Catholic and Orthodox collections contain additional books, and Orthodox collections also vary. The number and identity of the included books concern canon; the language used to render them concerns translation.

That distinction explains why two Bibles can differ without either being fabricated. One may contain a book absent from the other. Two may contain the same books but express a verse differently because they use different translations. A verse may also differ because translators made different judgments about the surviving ancient manuscripts. Those are related questions about the Bible's transmission, but they are not interchangeable.

The writings were copied, gathered, and translated over many centuries. Later lessons explain how scholars investigate that history and compare the evidence. For now, identify the kind of difference before making a judgment about it. A translation choice does not, by itself, determine which writings a community regards as Scripture.

## Ask who wrote it, for whom, and how
Luke's opening supplies a reason to ask who wrote an account, for whom, and with what purpose. He mentions earlier writings and transmitted testimony as well as his own investigation. Those details help explain how he presents his work to the reader. They also show why the beginning of a book can matter when you interpret a later passage.

The kind of writing matters too. Narrative, poetry, law, wisdom, letters, and apocalyptic visions communicate through different forms. They may make serious claims without functioning as modern scientific reports. Ask what the writer is doing before deciding what sort of explanation a passage should supply.

As you continue through the Learning Path, keep collection, translation, and genre distinct. The first concerns what belongs in the Bible you are using; the second concerns how you read its language; the third concerns how a particular passage communicates. Together they prevent common misunderstandings, such as treating a poem as a report or assuming every wording difference changes which books are Scripture.

## What you now know
You can now locate a passage by book, chapter, and verse, recognize several styles of reference, and read its surrounding context. You can also distinguish three reasons Bibles may differ: the books included in the canon, the wording of a translation, and the manuscript reading translators judge most likely to preserve the earlier text. Genre is a further question about how the passage communicates.

Christians broadly recognize that Scripture came through human writers and communities that wrote, gathered, copied, and transmitted it. They disagree about how divine inspiration operated through that process, which books belong in the Old Testament, and how manuscript differences should be weighed. The third part of the Learning Path returns to manuscripts and translation in detail. Until then, ask what kind of difference you have encountered before deciding what it means.

## Glossary

**Canon.** A canon is the collection a community recognizes as Scripture. The question of canon concerns which writings belong in that collection, rather than the language in which someone reads them.

**Translation.** A translation renders a text from one language into another. Differences between translations may concern how the same ancient wording is expressed, while other differences arise from judgments about manuscripts.

**Genre.** Genre identifies a kind of writing and its usual purposes and conventions. Knowing the form helps explain what a passage is doing, whether it tells a story, offers a command, or communicates through an image.

## Deeper reading note

Copying, translation, and interpretation are distinct processes. Compare wording and manuscript evidence before deciding that a difference changes meaning. Neither deliberate corruption nor complete recovery of an original interpretation should be assumed.
