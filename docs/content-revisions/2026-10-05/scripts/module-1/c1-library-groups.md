# Meet the library: nine kinds of books

Lesson ID: c1-library-groups

**Objective:** Name the nine groups of the 66-book Protestant Bible, say what each group contains, and choose a sensible place to start reading in each.

**Primary reading:** Luke 24:44–45

## A library, not a book
The Bible brings together writings composed over many centuries by many authors. The 66-book Protestant collection used here has two main parts: the Old Testament, containing Israel's Scriptures, and the New Testament, containing the writings of the first Christians. Those books do not all use the same form or ask the same questions.

This library is arranged on nine shelves: Law, History, Wisdom and Poetry, Major Prophets, Minor Prophets, Gospels and Acts, Paul's Letters, General Letters, and Revelation. The shelves help you find a book and form a first expectation about how to read it. They do not replace attention to the passage itself.

In the reading, Jesus speaks of the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms. Notice that Scripture could be described in groups before this particular nine-shelf arrangement.

> **Read:** Luke 24:44–45 (BSB).

## The Old Testament shelves
The Law contains Genesis through Deuteronomy. These books tell Israel's founding story: creation, the ancestors, the Exodus from Egypt, and the covenant at Sinai. The stories provide the setting for the laws, and the laws describe the life of a covenant community in its own world. Reading them well means relating a command to that community rather than copying every rule directly into modern life. Begin with Genesis 1–3 and 12, followed by Exodus 1–3 and 20.

The History books run from Joshua through Esther. They follow Israel in the land through judges, kings, division, exile, and return. Their accounts interpret events as well as reporting them: repeated patterns of faithfulness and failure help explain what the writers think the events mean. Begin with 1 Samuel 16–17, 2 Kings 17, and Nehemiah 8.

Wisdom and Poetry contains Job through Song of Songs. These books bring together prayers, songs, sayings, and reflections on suffering, love, and the way life works. Read slowly enough to notice images and the relationship between paired lines. The second line of a Hebrew poem may deepen the first, so both belong to the meaning. Begin with Psalms 1, 23, and 139, Proverbs 1, and Job 38.

The Major Prophets, Isaiah through Daniel, are longer books. The Minor Prophets, Hosea through Malachi, are twelve shorter books; minor describes their length, not their importance. The prophets address people before, during, and after the exile with warnings, appeals, and promises. Ask whom they address and which injustice they name before looking for predictions. Begin with Isaiah 1 and 40, Jeremiah 31, Amos 5, Micah 6, and the four chapters of Jonah.

## The New Testament shelves
The Gospels and Acts introduce Jesus' life, death, and resurrection and the early Church. The four Gospels share a central story while emphasizing different aspects of it for their readers. Acts continues the account begun in Luke. Begin with Mark, the shortest Gospel, then read Luke 15 and Acts 2.

Paul's Letters are grouped from Romans through Philemon. The General Letters run from Hebrews through Jude and address particular recipients or broader Christian audiences. These writings respond to real questions and problems. Find the writer, the readers, and the situation before extracting a teaching from a sentence. Begin with Philippians, Romans 5–8, James 1–2, and 1 John 4.

Revelation has its own shelf. It uses apocalyptic visions and symbols to address suffering, empire, and God's making all things new. Its images belong to a message for its first readers, so begin by asking what those images meant in their setting before considering their meaning for the future. Revelation 1 and 21–22 provide useful starting places.

## Labels, not boxes
The shelves are useful labels, but the writing within them is varied. A history book can contain a poem, a prophet can tell a story, and a letter can quote a hymn. Knowing a book's group gives you orientation; recognizing the form of a particular passage helps you interpret its claim.

That distinction explains the next lesson. We will move from the library's arrangement to the kinds of writing within its books and ask how each communicates meaning. A shelf can tell you where to begin without deciding every question about the text you find there.

## What you now know
You can now name the nine groups, describe what each contains, and choose a sensible place to begin reading. You also know why the first reading question changes from shelf to shelf: a law, a poem, a letter, and a vision communicate in different ways.

The same writings can also be arranged differently. Jewish Bibles group the Hebrew books into Law, Prophets, and Writings, while Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include additional books. This lesson has introduced the arrangement used here, rather than treating it as the only possible arrangement. Carry forward the question that will guide the next lesson: what kind of claim does this particular kind of writing make?

## Glossary

**Old Testament.** The Old Testament is the Christian name for the collection of Israel's Scriptures. Protestant Bibles contain 39 books in this collection, written mostly in Hebrew. Jewish readers share these writings but arrange and count them differently, and read them within a living Jewish tradition.

**New Testament.** The New Testament contains 27 writings about Jesus and the early Christian movement, composed in Greek. Its contents include four Gospels, Acts, letters, and Revelation, rather than one continuous account written in a single form.

**Pentateuch.** Pentateuch names the first five books of the Bible: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. This collection is also called the Torah or the Law; its laws occur within Israel's founding story.

**Canon.** A canon is the collection of books a community recognizes as Scripture. Protestant Bibles contain 66 books. Differences between Christian collections concern which writings are included, rather than simply how the same writings are translated.

**Epistle.** An epistle is a letter. Biblical letters address communities or other recipients and often respond to questions, disagreements, or conduct. Knowing the situation helps explain what the letter is doing.

## Additional questions

### Why nine groups and not two?

Old and New Testament tell you which collection a book belongs to. The nine groups tell you what kind of book you are holding, which changes how you read it.

### Do I have to read the Bible in order?

No. Many readers start with a Gospel, then Genesis and Exodus, then the Psalms. The shelf helps you know where you are wherever you begin.

### Are the Apocrypha missing?

Protestant Bibles do not include the additional books found in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles. The extra-credit module covers them, along with how the canon took shape.

## Deeper reading note

The group names used here follow common Protestant arrangement. Jewish Bibles arrange the same Hebrew books differently, into Law, Prophets, and Writings, and Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include additional books. Each book’s profile on the Bible page shows its themes, people, setting, and where to begin.
