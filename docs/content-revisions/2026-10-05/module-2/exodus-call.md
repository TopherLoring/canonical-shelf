## Exodus: hearing suffering and answering a call {#orient}
Module 2 begins with Exodus, the story of deliverance that becomes central to Israel’s identity. We begin at Moses’ call because it connects God’s concern for suffering people with a human being’s participation in their rescue. God first speaks of seeing, hearing, and knowing the people’s affliction. Oppression means the use of power to constrain or harm others; here its meaning comes into focus through the experience of an enslaved community. Their suffering receives attention in its own right, rather than appearing as an inconvenience for the rulers who benefit from it.



## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

As you read, follow God’s verbs before you look for Moses’ assignment: God has seen, heard, known, and come down. That order places the commission within an announced response to suffering. Then listen to Moses ask who he is to undertake the task and what name he should give the people. His questions help us see the relationship between the purpose of the call and the uncertainty of the person receiving it.



## Called with questions {#called-with-questions}
Moses receives a commission, a task entrusted to him for a stated purpose, and questions his ability to carry it out. The story keeps both elements together. God initiates the response to oppression while calling Moses to take part in it. Divine action and human participation therefore do not compete for a place in the narrative. Moses’ hesitation also remains visible: the significance of the task does not mean that the person undertaking it has become certain or no longer needs support.



## Presence and the divine name {#presence-and-name}
God answers Moses’ concern about adequacy with an assurance of presence. The answer directs attention from Moses’ qualifications to the relationship in which he will act. God’s self-identification also connects the encounter with the ancestors, so the commission belongs within Israel’s continuing story. Read the divine name reverently and attend to translation notes. Its linguistic and theological significance cannot be exhausted by treating a single English rendering as a complete explanation.

## Reading Exodus responsibly today {#today}
Christians can read Exodus as forming concern for people under oppression. Applying that concern responsibly requires identifying the actual harm and listening to the people experiencing it before deciding how to participate. The story begins with attention to suffering, so an application that ignores those people’s experience loses the very connection it claims to make. A present conflict does not become a direct reenactment of Exodus merely because someone gives it that name. Liberation language needs evidence and respect for the agency of those being helped.

## What you now know {#summary}
The call of Moses joins attention to an enslaved people’s suffering with the commission of a hesitant participant. God’s initiative gives the task its purpose, and Moses’ questions show that participation can include uncertainty. The assurance he receives is presence rather than a declaration that his own adequacy has become beyond question. Christians differ over how directly this story should shape modern politics. Canonical Shelf reads it as forming concern for people under oppression while requiring an argued connection to any present situation. Carry this question into the next lessons: what does deliverance require after people escape?
