## Davidic covenant: kingship becomes a line of hope {#orient}
The monarchy begins under warnings about the costs of power, yet 2 Samuel 7 introduces a lasting promise to David. This lesson follows how that promise connects dynasty, kingdom, temple, discipline, and later hope. David wants to build a house for God; God instead promises to build a house for David. House shifts from a building to a dynasty, a succession of rulers from one family. The wordplay links David’s temple ambition with royal succession while placing the initiative in God’s promise.



## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Notice the reversal between David’s intended house for God and the house promised to David. Read the promise with both meanings in view: a sanctuary building and a continuing family of rulers. The shift helps explain how the passage connects temple and kingship.



## A promise without idealizing kings {#promise-and-failure}
The promise names descendants, throne, kingdom, discipline, and enduring commitment. Discipline matters because the promise does not declare that every Davidic ruler will act justly. Later narratives expose royal failure while preserving the memory of this commitment. Holding those elements together prevents the covenant from becoming an excuse to idealize kings or to treat their misconduct as approved.

## After the monarchy: a line of hope {#messianic-hope}
The monarchy’s collapse and exile interrupt Davidic rule, but Davidic language continues to shape prophetic and later Jewish hope. Messiah means anointed one; its use and the expectations associated with it vary across periods and communities. Royal hope connected with David becomes one important stream rather than the only possible meaning. The remembered promise can therefore become future-facing without making every later expectation identical.

## Jesus and the line of David {#jesus-and-david}
The New Testament presents Jesus through Davidic titles and genealogical claims. Those uses depend on the earlier promise and the hopes developed around it. Module 3 studies the diversity of messianic expectation and how the Gospels use this language, so the Christian connection can be explained without assuming that every earlier use already expressed it.



## What you now know {#summary}
God’s promise links David’s dynasty and kingdom with enduring commitment while retaining discipline for failure. Its memory survives individual kings’ misconduct and the monarchy’s collapse, becoming one important stream of messianic hope. Jewish and Christian readers draw on the promise while differing over its fulfillment; the New Testament identifies Jesus with Davidic language. Those later readings should preserve the promise’s earlier setting. Carry forward the question of what a lasting throne can mean when the kingdom itself falls.
