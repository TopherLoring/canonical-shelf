## Power, failure, exile, and return {#orient}
This lesson steps back across the movement from land and leadership through Jerusalem’s fall, exile, and the beginnings of return. Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings narrate conflict, faithfulness, and the use of power rather than merely listing rulers. Their stories ask what leadership does to people and whose suffering it produces. Appearing within that narrative does not make every leader or action exemplary. Connecting power’s costs with loss and rebuilding helps us keep communal consequences within the story’s larger arc.



## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Reread Samuel’s warning as a lens for the leadership stories that follow it. Attend to what royal power takes and compare the warning with the hopes and failures encountered in the accounts of kings. The earlier passage gains significance when read within that continuing movement.



## Power, hope, and failure {#power-and-hope}
The request for a king in 1 Samuel 8 meets a warning about labor, land, goods, and freedom being taken for royal purposes. Later stories contain both hopes attached to kingship and failures of rulers. Keep those elements together when evaluating power. Neither the presence of a ruler in Scripture nor a promise associated with kingship automatically endorses a modern ruler or political arrangement. An application must consider the consequences and explain the connection.

## Exile and lament {#exile}
Second Kings portrays Jerusalem’s destruction and the displacement of people. Exile includes the loss of homeland, institutions, and a familiar way of life. Psalm 137 voices grief and memory within that setting. Reading the lament beside the historical narrative matters because a sequence of events cannot by itself convey what communal catastrophe feels like. The poem keeps human experience visible within an account that might otherwise become only a timeline.



## Return and rebuilding {#return}
Ezra begins with permission to return and rebuild. Permission opens a possibility, but return neither removes all conflict nor restores the former world exactly. Rebuilding raises questions about worship, identity, neighbors, and communal responsibility. Its hope includes difficult practical work because a damaged community needs more than relief at an announced change in circumstances.

## The ethics of conquest and violence {#violence}
Conquest and violence in these writings need direct ethical attention. Context may explain an action’s setting without making the harm good. Distinguish reported acts, commands attributed to God, and later interpretations, while acknowledging that some passages remain morally difficult. Canonical Shelf examines present application through Jesus’ teaching, love of neighbor, and human dignity rather than authorizing a repetition of ancient violence.



## What you now know {#summary}
The land and leadership stories show both the hopes and costs of power, leading through Jerusalem’s destruction and exile to demanding work of return. Psalm 137 gives loss a human voice, and Ezra begins rebuilding without making the earlier world simply reappear. Violence throughout this arc remains an ethical concern that historical explanation does not erase. Historians differ in their confidence about parts of the biblical accounts; exile and return have substantial historical attestation. The next unit asks how Israel’s poets and sages speak about God when life does not follow the expected pattern.
