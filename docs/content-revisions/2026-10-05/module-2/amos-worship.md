## Amos: when worship and conduct collide {#orient}
Amos opens this unit’s reading of prophetic speech within the crises of Israel and Judah. A herdsman from Judah, he addresses the prosperous northern kingdom in the eighth century BCE. Amos 5 shocks its hearers by rejecting festivals, offerings, and songs associated with devotion. This lesson asks what that rejection accomplishes. The contrast with justice makes clear that religious activity cannot stand in for the community’s treatment of people. Public worship and communal conduct belong within the same prophetic examination.



## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Read the rejection of festivals, offerings, and songs in verses 21–23, then follow it into the demand of verse 24. Ask how the final demand explains the force of the preceding rejection. The two movements need to be interpreted together.



## Justice like flowing water {#justice-like-water}
The image of flowing water gives form to the demand for justice and righteousness. In this setting it calls for sustained communal conduct, rather than offering only an attractive description of nature. Its rhetorical force, the way the language confronts and persuades, depends on its contrast with rejected religious activity. What the community does to people is the concern placed against what it offers in worship.

## Not against worship, against worship without justice {#not-against-worship}
The passage does not establish that all singing and ritual are meaningless. It challenges worship separated from the justice expressed in its final demand. An interpretation should account for the contrast: taking the ritual rejection alone or quoting only the water image would lose the argument connecting them. The point is to examine worship and conduct together.



## Examining a community today {#examination}
A present community can ask whether its celebrations conceal unfair practices. Examine access, treatment of workers, use of money, and attention to people who are harmed. Amos gives such examination urgency because worship cannot substitute for justice. Any particular accusation still needs evidence; a prophet’s authority does not supply facts about a present situation by itself.

## What you now know {#summary}
Amos rejects worship that coexists comfortably with injustice and demands justice and righteousness that continue to flow. The relationship between the rejection and the demand identifies the target as worship cut off from how people are treated. Readers broadly agree about this concern while differing over its direct application to modern economic policy. Keep both the moral urgency and the need for evidence in view. The next lesson asks what God requires when larger religious offerings cannot purchase favor.
