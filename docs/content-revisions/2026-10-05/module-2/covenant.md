## Promise, liberation, and covenant {#orient}
God’s call to Abram connects a particular household with blessing that reaches beyond it. This lesson follows that promise through liberation and into covenant responsibility. A covenant is a committed relationship that involves promises and responsibilities; it helps us understand how belonging can shape a shared way of life. Reading the promise merely as a reward for exceptional people overlooks its outward direction. The call begins a long story in which trust, failure, conflict, and continuing relationship all have a place. Blessing beyond Abram’s family gives the beginning its wider purpose.



## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Read Genesis 12:1–3 at the beginning of this narrative movement. Listen especially for blessing and for the people beyond Abram’s own household who come within the promise’s horizon. That outward direction will help connect the promise with the later stories of liberation and communal responsibility.



## From promise to liberation {#liberation}
Exodus next portrays God attending to the suffering of an enslaved people and calling Moses into their liberation. Moses’ questions and limitations are kept within the account; neither his perfection nor an already perfected Israelite society explains the rescue. The narrative moves from oppression toward freedom before describing the responsibilities of communal life. This order matters because it prevents us from treating deliverance as a payment that Israel has already earned through flawless conduct.



## A communal vocation at Sinai {#vocation}
At Sinai, covenant gives Israel a communal vocation, a calling that concerns the people’s shared life. Instructions about worship and social conduct belong together within that relationship. Leviticus 19 makes the connection concrete through produce left for vulnerable people, honesty in dealings, justice, and love of neighbor. Holiness therefore concerns the treatment of others as well as worship. Those instructions explain how belonging to a covenant community carries responsibilities that reach into ordinary life.

## Reading ancient laws {#ancient-laws}
Ancient laws address particular communities and social arrangements. To apply them, distinguish what an instruction required in its original setting from the principle and present practice a later reader draws from it. Christians differ over the continuing application of particular Torah commands through Christ. Those differences require interpretation rather than a choice between treating the entire Torah as an unchanged modern rulebook and treating it as a relic without significance. Explain the relationship between setting, instruction, and application.



## Covenant and living Judaism {#judaism}
Judaism remains a living tradition with its own reading of covenant and Scripture. Christian interpretation should respect that continuing life, because Israel’s scriptures have significance beyond supplying clues for Christians. Follow the movement from promise through liberation to responsibility on its own terms. It gives us a way to ask how gratitude for rescue can become care for people with less power, rather than becoming only a community’s celebration of its own belonging.

## What you now know {#summary}
Promise, liberation, and covenant form a connected movement. Abram’s family is blessed with a purpose beyond itself; Israel is freed from slavery; and covenant joins belonging to responsibilities in worship and everyday justice. Canonical Shelf’s Statement of Faith holds that Jesus established a new covenant whose governing law is Love, and understands Christian moral responsibility through that covenant. Earlier covenants and laws remain important for understanding God’s relationship with humanity and the biblical story. Other Christian traditions draw these relationships differently, and later modules compare them. Carry forward the question of how gratitude for rescue becomes a way of life.
