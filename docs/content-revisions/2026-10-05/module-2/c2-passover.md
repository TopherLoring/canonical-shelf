## Passover: liberation remembered at a table {#orient}
Moses’ call began a response to slavery; Exodus 12 brings us to the night of departure. Passover names the festival that remembers Israel’s deliverance from Egypt, and this lesson asks how a meal can preserve that memory as part of a community’s identity. Exodus places the meal within the final confrontation with Pharaoh. Danger, departure, and rescue give the instructions their meaning. Later generations are told to observe the festival because this night is formative for the people they understand themselves to be. The meal belongs to that liberation story before later readers develop its imagery.



## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Read the passage with a household in view. Notice the lamb, the marked doorposts, and the clothing worn in readiness for travel. Follow those immediate instructions through to the command that the day be kept as a festival for future generations. The passage connects preparations for a particular night with an enduring practice of remembrance.



## The first Passover {#the-night}
At the first Passover, households prepare a lamb, mark their doors, and eat ready to leave. These actions are meaningful because departure is imminent; they are not simply directions for preparing a religious meal. The lasting observance preserves the memory of that setting. Begin with what the details do within Israel’s story before interpreting them through later Jewish or Christian uses. Otherwise, later symbolism can replace the event the passage asks its first audience to remember.

## A practice of memory {#memory}
A memorial is a practice through which a community keeps a formative event present in its identity. Passover does this by giving later generations a recurring participation in Israel’s received story. Remembering is consequently more than recalling information about an earlier event. Exodus language can return in later biblical discussions of rescue, covenant, worship, and belonging because the deliverance provides a shared point of reference. The meal makes that memory a communal practice rather than leaving it as an isolated history lesson.



## Later readings of Passover {#later}
The New Testament places important traditions about Jesus within Passover settings and draws on Passover imagery. Module 3 will examine those connections, including distinctions that a first introduction cannot settle. Christian interpretation is a later use of an already meaningful Jewish festival. It should therefore explain its connection to Exodus while preserving Passover’s Jewish meaning, rather than treating the earlier story as a code whose only significance lies in later events.

## What you now know {#summary}
Passover connects deliverance with ritual memory: a household meal eaten in readiness becomes an observance through which later generations remember their place in Israel’s story. This explains why the instructions combine immediate action with a lasting command. Jewish tradition continues to keep Passover, and the New Testament gives the festival an important place in the story of Jesus. Christians differ in how they draw the relationship between these settings. Canonical Shelf insists that those interpretations preserve the festival’s Jewish meaning. As you move forward, ask what a shared ritual of memory enables a community to do that a history lesson alone does not.
