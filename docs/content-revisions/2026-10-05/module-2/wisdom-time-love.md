## Ecclesiastes and Song: limits, delight, and poetry {#orient}
Ecclesiastes questions what lasts, while Song of Songs celebrates love and desire. This lesson considers how their poetry broadens wisdom beyond rules and certainty. Ecclesiastes 3 places contrasting times in pairs to show the range of human experience and the limits of control. A poetic list of actions is not automatically a command that each person should initiate them all. Recognizing the form helps distinguish what the poem describes from what an interpreter might tell a reader to do.



## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Read Ecclesiastes 3:1–8 as paired contrasts. Notice how the pairs present the range of life rather than a list of actions for you to carry out. Keep that distinction in view when interpreting any one line apart from its companions.



## Questions about what endures {#what-endures}
Ecclesiastes asks about work, gain, loss, and what endures. Individual observations belong within that wider inquiry, so one line should not be used to declare every human action pointless. The book unsettles easy promises of control while bringing attention back to the life actually available. Its questions about limits do not require the reader to erase the significance of everything experienced within those limits.

## Song of Songs: delight and desire {#song}
Song of Songs gives biblical space to the voices of lovers, desire, and delight. Read Song 2:8–13 in the additional passage with its springtime imagery in view. The poetry can be read attentively without denying embodied affection or treating every flower as a separate secret code. Its images contribute to the expression of love and desire within the poem.



## Allegorical readings {#allegory}
An allegorical reading relates the poem’s persons and images to another level of meaning. Jewish and Christian traditions have developed such readings of Song, but they should be identified as interpretations and connected to the language actually present. They should not silently replace it. Alongside Ecclesiastes, the poem widens the introduction to include limitation, affection, beauty, and attentive reading, rather than only rules and chronological events.

## What you now know {#summary}
Ecclesiastes describes the breadth of human experience and questions human control without reducing life to pointlessness. Song of Songs gives room to embodied love and delight. Jewish and Christian traditions have long read Song in relation to God and Israel or Christ and the Church, while most modern scholars begin with love poetry. Canonical Shelf presents both approaches and labels the allegorical readings as interpretation. The next unit asks how prophets speaking into crisis combine judgment with hope.
