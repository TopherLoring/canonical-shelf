# Module 2 editorial review

These 33 revised lesson scripts are review drafts only. Their source is the existing Module 2 lesson set in `content/pathway/lessons/`, with membership and sequence taken from `source-inventory.json`. No runtime curriculum, lesson identity, activity, record, or publication has been changed.

The revisions retain existing subjects and historical detail. They explain the reasoning within existing claims: why rescue precedes covenant responsibility, why ritual distinctions matter, why a narrator's description does not establish endorsement, and why an original audience constrains later application. They do not introduce new topics, lessons, speculative etymologies, or new application examples. Opening epigraphs that restated the first paragraph have been absorbed into the script rather than repeated above it. Each opening establishes the setting and direction once. Existing glossary terms now have complete explanatory sentences, using context already supplied by their source lessons.

## Actual sequence, overlaps, and remaining gaps

The Exodus-to-Sinai progression is clear. `covenant` recaps Moses and liberation immediately after three Exodus lessons, then anticipates Sinai and Leviticus before they are studied separately. Its distinctive contribution is the connection to Abram and outward blessing; the repeated rescue material can feel like a second beginning if each recap is read as a fresh introduction. The draft gives the recap an explicit argumentative role without moving or retiring any material.

The sanctuary sequence is strong: portable space, Ark, roles and offerings, annual rites, then sacred time. The calendar nevertheless repeats the earlier Passover lesson. Here the distinctive task is comparison with Weeks and Booths and the later Gospel/Acts calendar, so the earlier departure story is treated as known context. Detail remains in both lessons because the task does not authorize removal.

`kings-accountability` and `kingdom-exile` both read Samuel's warning; the latter then surveys catastrophe and return before those subjects receive focused treatment much later. `land-judges` and `kingdom-exile` both discuss conquest ethics. These are real spiral overlaps, not unrelated duplication, but the broad recap's position creates a long pause between its exile/return claims and their primary readings. Nothing has been reordered.

`wisdom` surveys the subjects immediately developed by `psalms-lament`, `proverbs-discernment`, `job-listening`, and `wisdom-time-love`. Psalm 13 is read twice consecutively. The survey's unique role is distinguishing the tools; the focused lesson traces the poem and practices prayer. Their openings now use those different tasks rather than both announcing that honest grief belongs in Scripture.

`amos-worship` and the later `prophets` return to the same Amos reading. The latter also repeats Micah and anticipates Jeremiah's letter. It functions as a method-level synthesis rather than another first introduction, but its placement after three restoration/hope lessons interrupts the political and prophetic movement. `return-rebuilding` and `c2-restoration-temple` overlap in return and incomplete restoration, yet the order first presents the Temple foundation and later returns to Ezra's initial proclamation. That movement needs reader awareness; the scripts clarify each passage's role without silently changing sequence.

The module title promises the Near Eastern world, but the current set foregrounds Israel's canonical narrative and ritual institutions. Broad comparative Near Eastern history, law, material culture, and composition history are not developed here. Genesis is represented through Abram's promise rather than a sustained creation/ancestral sequence; the transition from Solomon to Babylon is largely a recap rather than sustained divided-kingdom history. These are observations about existing coverage, not authorization to add units, historical digressions, or new IDs.

Several protected drawers and deeper notes still refer to Course 3–6, although the inventory uses the current four-module structure. Their content is preserved as requested. This is a real reference-maintenance issue for a later approved metadata/apparatus pass. In particular, the surviving Course 5 references concern interpretive relationships and Course 6 references concern theological frameworks; those legacy numbers should not be mistaken for new modules. Some prose references have been phrased by subject or current module without changing the teaching they point toward.

## Factual clarification and evidence limits

`torah-neighbor` formerly said each instruction ends with the same reason, “I am the LORD.” The selected verses use the declaration repeatedly, but it is not the ending of every individual instruction. The draft says repeated declaration, preserving the covenant connection without overstating its frequency. This is checked directly against the selected reading, Leviticus 19:9–18.

`land-judges` formerly included crying out within its instruction to trace the selected Judges 2:6–19 passage. The selected summary emphasizes wrongdoing, oppression, raised deliverers, compassion at groaning, and renewed failure; the wider book makes cries for rescue explicit. The draft distinguishes the selected passage from the wider cycle. This preserves the existing assessment's sequence without claiming every element is explicitly stated in those verses.

`c2-exile-temple-loss` formerly described the final destruction as well attested “including in Babylonian records,” without distinguishing events or sources. The [British Museum's Babylonian Chronicle catalog](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1896-0409-51) identifies its Jerusalem campaign as **597 BCE**, which is an earlier capture than the final destruction conventionally dated here to 586 BCE. The draft preserves the wider historical attestation while explicitly distinguishing it from a direct record of that final destruction. The conventional 586 date remains; the draft does not claim the catalog settles the 587/586 reconstruction question.

No new uncertain lexical derivations or historical episodes were introduced. Existing limitations remain visible for the divine name, crossing history, calf representation, sacrificial mechanics, tabernacle reconstruction, Ark traditions, festival development, royal promise, Temple history, deportation scale, messianic diversity, and interpretations of wisdom and prophetic hope. The scripts distinguish their canonical account from historical reconstruction and later theological reception. They are not a substitute for scholarly review of those disputed matters.
