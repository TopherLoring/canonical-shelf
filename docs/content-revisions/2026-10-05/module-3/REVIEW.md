# Module 3 editorial review

These 41 lesson scripts are review drafts. They revise the existing teaching and glossary entries without creating lessons, changing the course sequence, or publishing content to learners. Each opening now establishes the subject once in connected prose; the former epigraph is incorporated into the explanation rather than repeated above it. Subsequent paragraphs develop the existing claims and their reasons. Historical qualifications, doctrinal commitments, opposing interpretations, and existing examples remain visible.

## Preservation review

All 41 stable IDs, objectives, readings, section anchors, reading and visual markers, check JSON, reflection JSON, drawers, and deeper fields match the source. Front-matter fields other than glossary are unchanged. All 124 glossary definitions were individually revised into complete explanatory sentences while retaining their exact term keys. Checks retain their source order and section placement, including the checks between teaching sections in the Ascension and Pentecost lessons.

Teaching prose was compared separately from front matter, assessment JSON, reflection JSON, headings, and markers, with whitespace normalized. Every draft equals or exceeds its source teaching-prose character count. This is a preservation check, not evidence of final theological approval. The prose was authored by lesson; shared protected JSON was copied intact. No runtime source, learning identifier, doctrinal policy, completion rule, record, or deployment was changed.

## Coverage and actual sequence issues

The first fifteen lessons give the module a coherent chronological and institutional bridge: Persia, Greek influence, the Maccabean crisis, Hasmoneans, Rome and Herod, Temple and local community, Jewish groups, resistance, and diverse hopes. They establish the background for entering the Gospels rather than treating the interval after the Hebrew Scriptures as empty.

The remaining lessons combine Gospel and early-community reading with interpretation workshops. They preserve existing coverage, but the inventory exposes several sequence questions:

- The listed unit `c5.transmission` has no lesson. The translation lesson supplies a manuscript exercise, but that does not make the empty transmission unit authored. No replacement ID or new lesson was invented.
- `project-interpret` calls itself lab 2 and assumes the whole-letter profile has been completed, while `project-observe` and `project-apply` are listed in Module 4. This is a genuine prerequisite mismatch in the current inventory, not a prose problem to disguise.
- `c4-scripture-fulfillment` and `c5-typology-allusion` use the same Matthew 2 reading and Hosea example. The first introduces fulfillment, including Jeremiah; the second classifies textual reuse. Their distinction is defensible, but the later lesson repeats substantial groundwork and needs an explicit sequence rationale during structural review.
- `gospel-comparison`, `c5-synoptic-problem`, and `c5-authorship-composition` address related questions. The first teaches comparative reading; the later pair address sources and attribution. Luke 1:1–4 recurs in the first and last. Preserve these different objectives, but check whether their considerable separation supports the intended spiral.
- `jesus-world` introduces kingdom, discipleship, Beatitudes, and parables before dedicated lessons develop them. `practices` revisits Communion and prayer, and `early-church` previews body imagery and favoritism before their separate lessons. These are survey/detail overlaps rather than evidence that the detailed lessons should be retired.
- `audience` and `communion-table` read adjacent portions of 1 Corinthians 11 with substantial shared concern. Their respective objectives are recovering the conversation and connecting Communion with conduct. The sequence should signal that difference rather than simply repeat a generalized welcome introduction.
- `context`, `audience`, and `application` arrive after substantial Gospel teaching. They can provide deliberate practice after earlier hermeneutics, but their late position is a review question because those skills are assumed throughout the module.
- Source drawers and deeper fields contain retired Course 2/4/5/6 pointers and Unit 11 references. Their exact preservation prevents silent metadata changes; several prose references now describe the relevant later study without relying on the retired course numbering. Structural review should resolve the remaining references centrally.

## Factual review and evidence limits

No new historical theory, date, denominational identity, or etymological excursus was added. The drafts preserve the source’s major historical claims and its uncertainty labels. The main corrections are explanatory distinctions: Herod renovates an existing Second Temple, diaspora continues after return, Greek-speaking identity is not equivalent to non-Jewish identity, and historical reconstruction is not direct textual testimony. These were already source teaching points, now made clearer rather than newly asserted.

The existing translation workshop was checked against the publisher’s [NET notes on 1 John 5](https://classic.net.bible.org/bible.php?book=1Jo&chapter=5&mode=print). Its textual-critical note supports distinguishing the longer heavenly-witness clause from an English synonym choice and treating its Greek attestation as late and limited. The draft does not infer translators’ motives or make this single reading decide the whole doctrine of the Trinity. The source mentions a passage “below” and linked NET notes, but the Markdown has only the principal `::reading` marker. These drafts preserve that source structure. Any runtime provision of the additional passage or linked note should be verified separately before learner publication.

The resistance lesson’s caution about comparing sources remains important. Josephus places Theudas under Fadus in [Antiquities 20.97](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0146%3Abook%3D20%3Asection%3D97), while Acts 5 presents its own sequence of remembered movements. The primary accounts should not be silently harmonized into one party or a resolved chronology. The draft retains the source’s limited comparison without adding an explanation of this historical question.

Qumran’s relationship to the Essenes, boundaries of Zealot terminology, the dating and interpretation of Daniel, Gospel source relationships, authorship, and pseudonymity retain their existing qualifications. These drafts are not independent resolutions of those scholarly disputes. The historical survey also remains broad: it does not establish a precise constitutional arrangement for every decade of Hasmonean or Roman rule.

The source’s open-table, LGBTQ-affirming, fully divine and fully human, grace, baptism, and pastoral-care positions remain explicit where already taught. Opposing Communion and baptism understandings are preserved without caricature. Assessments continue to measure interpretation and reasoning rather than personal assent. The next review concerns accuracy and teaching quality of the rewritten scripts; publication and pagination remain separate decisions.
