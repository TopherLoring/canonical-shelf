## Letters: reading a correction as a correction {#orient}

An epistle is a letter, and its description of an action must be read alongside the writer’s judgment. James imagines or describes an assembly treating people differently because of visible wealth. Ordinary gestures, including a seating decision, communicate who is valued. The scene raises the question of how the letter evaluates that conduct, rather than recommending it simply by reporting it.



## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}

James criticizes favoritism. Isolating the seating scene from that criticism reverses the letter’s argument, because a harmful practice can be described precisely to confront it. The distinction between report and endorsement is essential to understanding what the writer asks of the audience.

Love of neighbor supplies the criticism’s ethical direction. A statement that everyone is welcome does not solve the problem if behavior still honors wealth and humiliates poverty. The principle must be compared with what people actually experience. James’ example makes that comparison possible by showing how social judgment takes practical form.



## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}

Use the same discipline when reading other letters: identify the recipients, the issue, the reasons, and the requested response. A correction tells us what concern the writer addresses, but it does not establish how common the problem is in every early Christian community. Keep the historical conclusion proportionate to the evidence. Then consider whether a present claim of equal welcome is supported by observable treatment of people with different means.
