## Passover and the passion: an old liberation story enters a new claim {#orient}

Passover remembers the Exodus and liberation within Jewish worship. The passion traditions occur in that festival world, and the Synoptic Gospels frame Jesus’ final meal with the disciples in relation to Passover. Bringing the earlier Exodus study into the scene makes the meal’s inherited meaning visible. Christian interpretation adds a claim about Jesus within this history rather than making Passover an unexplained Christian term.



## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}

Jesus interprets the bread and cup through his body and covenant blood. Luke also uses new-covenant language. That wording connects the meal with prophetic restoration, including Jeremiah 31, as well as with sacrifice and covenant imagery. These relationships help explain why the meal gathers several strands of Scripture rather than relying on a single isolated reference.

The Gospels and Paul preserve related but differing meal traditions. Comparing their wording and framing matters because a combined account can obscure the differences. The later Christian practice called Communion, Eucharist, or the Lord’s Supper grows from these traditions and early communal meals. Eucharist is a traditional name associated with thanksgiving. No one proof text by itself supplies the whole history of the practice.



## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}

Christians interpret Jesus’ death through Passover, covenant, sacrifice, and liberation patterns. This is a Christian interpretive claim. It does not cancel Passover’s continuing Jewish meaning or practice. Keep the Exodus and Jeremiah backgrounds visible alongside the later claim, and preserve the Gospel differences rather than forcing them into an identical chronology.
