## Investigate a translation difference {#orient}

A difference between English Bibles needs a specific question before it needs a verdict. It may concern translation, underlying manuscript wording, or interpretation. Translators work from ancient-language texts and choose how words, sentences, idioms, and style will communicate. A word can have several possible meanings, and copying an expression’s order mechanically may obscure what it means. These decisions explain why different English wording can sometimes express a closely related idea.



## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

## Locate the claim in context {#locate-the-claim-in-context}

Compare the opening of 1 Corinthians 13:4. The King James Version says “Charity suffereth long, and is kind”; the Berean Standard Bible says “Love is patient, love is kind.” Here charity is not restricted to giving money. The surrounding verses describe patient, kind conduct within relationships. Those details help us understand how changed English wording can convey a closely related meaning.

A translation question asks how to render an underlying expression. A textual question asks which wording the surviving manuscript witnesses best support when they differ. An interpretive question asks what the passage means in context or how it applies today. A comparison can involve several layers, but one layer does not automatically establish another. Different English words alone do not show that the underlying manuscripts contain different wording.



## Keep the setting visible {#keep-the-setting-visible}

We cannot simply inspect the original handwritten documents. Textual scholars compare surviving witnesses, their dates and relationships, and patterns that might explain variations. A variant footnote makes evidence and editorial judgment available to the reader. Its presence does not establish that editors hid or invented doctrine. Conversely, confidence in Scripture does not put every translation choice beyond examination.

Canonical Shelf treats Scripture as inspired and authoritative while permitting critical study of transmission and interpretation. Begin an investigation with the paragraph, then compare translations and consult their methods and notes. Limit the conclusion to what this evidence supports. If an original-language question remains uncertain, acknowledge that limit. Knowing Greek or Hebrew can assist the investigation, but it is not a prerequisite for beginning these distinctions responsibly.



## Read closely {#read-closely}

In the manuscript workshop, read 1 John 5:6–8 below and compare the linked NET notes. At 5:7, the KJV includes “in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one.” The BSB reads “For there are three that testify:” and continues with Spirit, water, and blood in 5:8. This difference involves a clause in the source text, not simply updated English vocabulary.

NET labels the issue tc, for textual criticism, rather than tn, for a translation note. The note reports late and limited Greek support for the longer reading and explains the decision to exclude it from the main text. Read the reported manuscript evidence separately from the editors’ conclusion. The note makes their judgment inspectable; it does not reveal a translator’s motive by itself. Nor does a judgment about this reading’s originality alone settle the doctrine of the Trinity, which requires examination across passages.
