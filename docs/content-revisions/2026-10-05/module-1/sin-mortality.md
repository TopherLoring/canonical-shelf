## Rupture, mortality, and the tree of life {#orient}
Genesis 3 describes hiding, blame, consequences, and the closed way to the tree of life. Christians have interpreted this rupture in several different ways, so Canonical Shelf's position is stated before the details are examined.

**Canonical Shelf's own position.** The first human sin brought mortality into the human condition. Humanity inherits its consequences, including mortality and a damaged condition, but does not inherit the personal guilt of those who acted before us. Within that condition, each person eventually sins through choices of their own and is responsible for those choices.

Many Western Christian traditions, particularly those influenced by Augustine, also teach inherited guilt. That account belongs to the later interpretation of the passage rather than being something this lesson settles from Genesis alone. Keeping these positions distinct lets you examine their claims while reading how the story depicts the rupture.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Follow God's questions and the answers the humans give. Notice how they direct attention elsewhere, then trace the consequences into work, relationships, and life itself. The conversation connects the damaged relationship with the conditions described afterward.

## Hiding and blame {#hiding}
The humans now respond to God with hiding and fear. When asked about their actions, they point toward others. These responses depict a rupture in trust and accountability before the story turns to toil and death.

Sin is consequently presented as more than breaking a detached rule. The choice affects how the humans relate to God and to one another. The earlier picture of trust helps explain why their fear and blame matter within the account.

## Consequences, not commands {#consequences}
The consequences reach through shared life: painful labor, strained relationships, and return to dust. Read these descriptions as an account of damage rather than instructions to create it. The story's depiction of suffering does not make suffering an ideal readers should preserve.

The same distinction applies to domination. Reporting a broken relationship does not command later people to reproduce it. Reading what the text describes and what it prescribes as separate questions prevents a consequence of rupture from being mistaken for a moral requirement.

## The closed way to the tree of life {#tree-of-life}
Expulsion closes the way to the tree of life. Canonical Shelf understands the loss in relation to humanity's intended enduring life with God. Mortality and a damaged condition become part of what later people inherit.

That teaching does not treat inherited personal guilt as settled by this scene, nor does it extend the claim about human mortality to an assertion that animals could not die before sin. The distinctions matter because the account of lost access concerns a particular part of the story. Later questions need their own reasons rather than being assumed within it.

## Judgment and care together {#judgment-and-care}
God clothes the humans within the scene of judgment. Care remains present alongside consequences and expulsion. Reading those details together does not make the rupture trivial; it recognizes that judgment does not remove every sign of provision.

Later Christian teaching connects this story with Christ's saving work. Such a connection should explain the details of Genesis rather than replacing them with a later theory. The hiding, blame, consequences, lost access, and clothing remain part of the account an interpretation needs to address.

## What you now know {#summary}
Genesis 3 depicts sin through damaged trust and relationships. It describes consequences without commanding them, closes access to the tree of life, and includes God's provision of clothing. Canonical Shelf teaches that humanity inherits mortality and a damaged condition, while personal guilt belongs to a person's own wrongdoing.

Christians disagree sharply about that distinction. Many Western traditions shaped by Augustine teach inherited guilt; Eastern traditions and others emphasize inherited mortality. The lesson has identified those differences without claiming Genesis alone settles all of them. When later writers connect the scene with Christ, ask which details they rely on and how the connection explains those details.
