## A living world entrusted to our care {#orient}
Genesis introduced human responsibility within a good creation. Psalm 104 gives sustained attention to the living world itself, praising its variety and the provision on which its creatures depend. This lesson asks how that praise informs practical care.

The poem notices creatures whose value is not exhausted by their usefulness to a human reader. That attention invites appreciation before turning creation into a list of tasks or resources to consume. Care follows from recognizing a world of dependent life as valuable, and its practical effect needs to be examined rather than assumed.

## Read the passage with the question in view {#read}
::reading

Notice the creatures the psalm names and what it says sustains them. Read their variety alongside their dependence so that both remain part of the reason for considering creation's care.

## Dependence and renewal {#dependence}
The poem describes creatures relying on provision and life-giving breath. It also names death and renewal, holding the abundance of life together with its dependence. Those are theological claims made through poetry.

They are not presented as a modern scientific model of how living systems change. Reading the poem's claims about God does not require ignoring natural processes. It means attending to the relationship the poem describes between the creatures, their life, and the one on whom they depend.

## From praise to practice {#praise-to-practice}
Creation care is an application of that value and dependence, read alongside humanity's responsibility in Genesis. The reasoning is that a world received as good and filled with dependent life deserves care rather than being treated as disposable.

The psalm does not supply a complete environmental policy. A practical proposal requires further evidence about its consequences, the resources involved, and the people most affected. The passage provides a reason to care; investigation helps decide how to carry out that care responsibly.

## Start with one shared resource {#one-resource}
Begin with one shared resource, such as water, food, energy, or a neighborhood space. Identify waste or harm, ask who bears its cost, and choose a realistic improvement. These questions connect the lesson's concern with something that can actually be examined.

Then investigate whether the action helps. Good intentions do not by themselves establish good effects, particularly when a plan imposes costs on other people. Responsible care includes learning from those effects and revising the response when needed.

## What you now know {#summary}
Psalm 104 values living creatures beyond their usefulness to humans and portrays their life as dependent on God. Creation care applies that vision together with the responsibility introduced in Genesis. Its practical expression should be judged by real effects as well as intentions.

Christians broadly affirm creation's goodness while differing about how central its care is to Christian life and which policies should follow. The poem supplies value and dependence, rather than selecting a policy for every setting. Carry forward the practical question that follows from this distinction: what evidence would show that an action actually helps?
